# Thematic Design Language — The Matrix in Play

The Matrix should function as an invisible design grammar long before players learn that an equation exists.

When authoring a floor, faction, quest, or major encounter, identify which link is being pressured:

| Link | Play Question |
|---|---|
| **Possibility** | Are there genuinely different futures available? |
| **Choice** | Must someone commit rather than defer forever? |
| **Difference** | What makes the available people, beliefs, plans, or outcomes meaningfully distinct? |
| **Conflict** | Which legitimate or understandable interests cannot all be satisfied at once? |
| **Motion** | What changes because the party acted—or refused to act? |
| **Time** | What becomes irreversible as the clock advances? |
| **History** | What account of the past will survive the event? |
| **Memory** | Who remembers, forgets, suppresses, or rewrites what happened? |
| **Identity** | What must a character or faction become to continue? |
| **Will** | What will someone choose when the cost is real? |
| **Life** | What makes continued existence worth preserving? |

## Encounter Rule

Do not turn the Matrix into eleven themed rooms with labels on the doors. It is a design test, not a puzzle key.

A good Decennium encounter usually pressures several links at once. A timed rescue can create possibility, force choice, reveal competing values, create conflict, advance history, and permanently change how a faction remembers the party.

## The Clock

The 100-day Opening is part of this language. A choice without a deadline can remain hypothetical. A choice under the Horn becomes an act of will.

## Surface Consequences

The Matrix does not stop at Floor 1. Loot claims, funerals, discoveries, debts, marriages, wars, guild policy, scholarship, and propaganda can continue the same chain on the surface. Every major recovered object should be evaluated for what new choices it creates outside the dungeon.
