The Director's Crucible
The core Director's manual for Decennium Descent: running Aethel, the Decennial Opening, and the hundred-floor descent.
Complete Director Edition — July 2026
Companion volumes: Codex of the Heartcell (monsters and ecology), Instruments of the Weave (downtime, craft, research, and expedition tools), Chronicle of the Decennial Opening (setting gazetteer), and Director Source Annex (extended secret cosmology and campaign documents). This book does not reprint those works in full; when a subject belongs elsewhere, it points you to the appropriate volume.
Rules authority: The Player Rulebook governs character creation, class features, spells, and other player-facing procedures. This book governs world presentation, encounter staging, campaign clocks, consequences, and adjudication. When the two books genuinely conflict, use the Player Rulebook for the current session, record the contradiction, and resolve it through errata before the next session rather than changing either rule in the middle of play.
Director-secret material: This manual contains the Silent Ones, the Heartcell, and the true architecture beneath the public Crucible myth. That material is written for you, the Director, only. It is marked with a lock icon (🔒) and the words Director-secret. Never leak it into player-facing recaps, handouts, or newcomer guides. The world of Aethel does not know it. Most player characters never will. A few, if they earn it, might.
How to Use This Book
Read Part I before your first session. Read Part II before you stage your first floor. Keep Part III open during combat and rules adjudication. Part IV covers table management and continuing play. Parts V and VI are the Director’s construction and campaign engines. Part VII is a complete, ready-to-run Tier 1 Opening. Part VIII and the appendices are designed for repeated table reference.
This book assumes you have read the Player Rulebook. It does not re-teach character creation, class features, or spell lists. It teaches you how to run a table that uses them. Director is Decennium Descent's term for the Game Master.

PART I — MASTER OF THE OPENING
Chapter 1 — What a Director Is
You are not an opponent. You are not a narrator reading a script, either. A Director sits at the exact point where the Pact's dissonance, the players' ambition, and a hundred floors of engineered temptation all meet — and decides, moment to moment, what happens when they collide.
Your job has four parts. First, you hold the world's memory: what the party has seen, who they have wronged, and which floor's wards are still smoking from last session. Second, you hold the clocks: the 100-day Opening, the Horn's halfway toll, and the hour a torch gutters out. Third, you hold the monsters, the Guardians, and the dungeon's appetite; you decide how hard it pushes back. Fourth, and most easily forgotten, you hold the bait: every glittering item, every rumor of a Custodian's diary, and every reason to go one floor deeper than is wise.
Tone. Decennium Descent runs on a grim liturgy, not a grimdark one. Heaven and Hell signed a Pact instead of ending the argument by force; that tension is supposed to feel unresolved, procedural, almost bureaucratic in how carefully it is managed — and then suddenly, personally catastrophic when it breaks down over one party's actions. Dissonance — war, ambition, grudge, appetite — is not a moral failing in this setting. It is fuel the setting's oldest machinery quietly needs. Play that irony forward rather than lecturing about it: let NPCs bless conflict without knowing why, let temple doctrine praise "the trial of struggle," and let your players notice the pattern themselves long before any Custodian confirms it.
The bait is not a trick on your players — it is a trick on their characters. Loot in Decennium Descent is never "just treasure." It is placed to be found, carried up, sold, worn, fought over, and eventually returned to the depths by the next expedition drawn by the story of what it did to the last one. Play every artifact as a small, patient recruiter.
What you owe the table. Consistency of consequence, fairness of information, and honesty about provisional material. During playtesting, say plainly when a rule or creature is still being tuned. A Director who owns uncertainty earns more trust than one who bluffs confidence.
What you do not owe the table. You do not owe victory. You do not owe every mystery an answer this Cycle. You do not owe the Silent Ones' name to a room full of new players at session zero — see Chapter 8 for exactly how far the myth goes before you have to invent nothing further.
Chapter 2 — The Shape of a Session
A session is a slice of Opening time, and it should feel like one. Open with stakes — why this floor, why today, what happens if the party does nothing. Close with consequences that a player can write on their sheet: loot, injuries, a map fragment, a faction's opinion shifting, hours burned toward the Horn.
Session architecture, beat by beat.
1. Cold open. State the day-count and hour-count inside the Opening out loud. Players should always be able to answer, "How deep are we into the 100-day Opening?" without asking.
2. Assignment. If the Expedition split teams last time (see Chapter 4), resolve or continue their tasks before the spotlight moves to combat.
3. The push. The floor, room, negotiation, or hazard that is this session's main event. Most of your prep time should live here.
4. The reckoning. Loot, wounds, hours spent, and any faction or Pact-adjacent consequence get named at the table before the session ends — not left to be inferred later.
5. The hook. One clear thread into next session, even if it is only a rumor.
Spotlight across five teams. The Player Rulebook's Expedition structure (Chapter 4 of this book) exists precisely so that a session is not only combat. Rotate attention deliberately: while the Combat team clears a room, cut to Reconnaissance finding the safer route, or Research translating a wall inscription that will matter in twenty minutes, or Resource haggling with a haven trader, or Support stabilizing yesterday's wounded. A session where only the Combat team spoke for three hours is a session that under-used the game.
Resolution methods, matched to pace. Use Quick Resolution (a single check, a stated outcome) for routine foraging, maintenance, and travel. Use Scene-Based play for a discovery or negotiation that deserves dialogue and a decision point. Use Parallel Play when teams split — narrate in short cuts, a few minutes each, so no team's player checks out for half an hour. Use Between-Session resolution for research or craft chains that would otherwise stall the table (see Chapter 24).
Optional tracking tools. A digital or paper session tracker may manage hit points, initiative, conditions, Resonance Points, Oracle Revelation Charges, and Opening hours. The Director must still verify death saves, Hit Dice spent on short rests, concentration, and expedition team assignments. Announce the critical clocks and conditions aloud; the table should never depend on an application to understand the danger.
Chapter 3 — The Cycle and the Clocks (A World in Motion)
Aethel does not sit still between sessions, and it does not sit still across the ten years between Cycles either. This chapter is your master clock reference — pin it somewhere visible.
The three clocks that matter, in order of size:

The Cycle. Once a decade, the Shardspire activates and the Decennium's hundred floors accept mortal expeditions again. Between Cycles, the dungeon is sealed; only rumor, old maps, and the last Cycle's survivors carry knowledge forward. A Director should treat each new Cycle as a chance to reshuffle: a Gate Guardian's domain may have shifted in emphasis, a faction that dominated the last Cycle may have fallen from favor, a haven that was safe may now be a ruin. Name the Cycle after whoever or whatever most shaped it — "the Cycle of the Broken Warded Gate," "the Third Choralith Cycle" — and let players feel the weight of history stacking up.
The Opening — a calendar, not just a countdown. One hundred days is brief enough to feel desperate and long enough to develop a shape. Treat it in three movements: the rush (hours 0–480, roughly days 1–20, when expeditions race for early floors and fresh rumors), the grind (hours 480–1200, roughly days 21–50, when serious companies push depth and the surface absorbs the first loot and casualties), and the reckoning (hours 1200–2400, roughly days 51–100, everything after the Horn). Mark visible beats on the calendar: a haven running short of supplies, a market reacting to a windfall, or a rival Expedition returning in triumph or ruin.
The Horn — the point of no return. At hour 1200, day 50, the Horn sounds at the exact midpoint of the Opening. After it, known extraction routes become less reliable, rescue contracts thin out, and NPC allies begin making final decisions about whether to push deeper or turn upward. The Horn does not seal the dungeon by itself, but it ends the illusion that every Expedition can still recover from one more mistake.
The seal. At hour 2400, day 100, the Decennium seals. Anyone still inside is not automatically killed; survivors may be trapped until the next Cycle, ten years later. Telegraph the closing repeatedly. A seal that arrives without warning is not tension; it is an ambush by the rules.
Director canon for the tabletop game is a 2400-hour Opening (100 days) with the Horn at hour 1200 (day 50). Do not compress the Opening to 240/120 — that figure was a typo. Use smaller local clocks for patrol resets, torch life, collapsing rooms, or a single delve, but give those clocks distinct names so they are never confused with the Horn.
Chapter 4 — Expeditions at the Table
This is the Player Rulebook's own structure, not a Director invention — treat it as binding, and use this chapter to actually run it, because most tables under-use it.
The cap and why it exists. An Expedition is capped at twelve characters, and that cap describes an organization, not a combat party. A table of four players is a perfectly legal, tiny Expedition. A table that wants to field twenty-four characters is not one oversized Expedition — it is two or three separate Expeditions coordinating, each still respecting the twelve-seat cap, sharing a base camp and staggered combat teams if the story calls for it. Do not let "the party" and "the Expedition" become synonyms in your head; a full Expedition includes hired specialists, hangers-on, and NPCs the players never personally control.

Base camps. As an Expedition advances, it can establish a Temporary Camp (a night's shelter), a Fortified Outpost (defensible, holds gear and wounded safely), a Research Station (bonus to Research team checks on nearby floor lore), or a Forward Base (extends how deep Combat can push before needing to retreat). Advancement — reputation, better tools, base improvements, NPC specialist allies, a logistics network back to the surface — should be visible and earned, not handed out for free.
Resolving non-combat teams without stalling the table. Use Quick Resolution for a single check with an obvious outcome ("Resource rolls Survival to forage; on a success, camp eats well tonight"). Use Scene-Based play when a Research find or Recon discovery should matter dramatically. Use Parallel Play, cutting briefly between teams, when the party has split for the session. Use Between-Session resolution for anything that would otherwise burn table time on math (see Chapter 24).
Chapter 5 — Creating Adventures and Expedition Arcs
An "adventure" in Decennium Descent is rarely a single dungeon crawl — it's an arc across some slice of an Opening, built from a hook, an obstacle, and a consequence that seeds the next arc.
Where hooks come from. The strongest hooks in this setting are never "a wizard needs an errand." They come from the machine's own appetite: a rumor that a Custodian's private ledger survived on Floor 44; a rival Expedition's disastrous return with an item everyone now wants; a faction offering absurd money for something specific, for reasons they won't explain; a haven under siege from something that came up from a floor rather than in from the wilds. Loot-as-bait means your hooks can honestly be about greed — the world is built to make greed reasonable.
Three shapes of arc.
Dungeon arcs are vertical: a floor or Gate needs clearing, mapped, or its guardian confronted. These lean on Chapter 4's Expedition structure and Part II's gazetteer.
Wilderness arcs happen on the surface between Openings, or on long overland routes to a distant Gate. These lean on Resource and Recon teams, travel attrition, and faction territory.
Urban/political arcs happen in havens and surface cities: guild rivalries, temple politics, black-market fencing of dungeon-touched goods, or a Custodian-adjacent conspiracy that a Research team stumbles into. These lean on Reputation (Chapter 12) and NPCs (Chapter 13).
Most real campaigns braid all three across an Opening: a wilderness push to reach a distant Gate, a dungeon arc once inside, an urban arc when the loot comes home and changes the politics that sent the party out in the first place.
Villains, patrons, and mysteries. A villain in this setting works best when their goal is legible dissonance — they want a war, a grudge kept alive, a rival Expedition destroyed, a secret kept buried past its safe date. A patron works best when their motive has a visible gap the players can eventually question (why do they want this specific artifact; why do they always fund expeditions that end violently). A mystery works best when its answer, once found, quietly rhymes with the Director-secret cosmology in Chapter 8 without ever stating it outright — the closer a mystery's true shape gets to "someone built this to be found," the better it fits Aethel.
Building an arc, step by step.
1. Name the want: what does the antagonist force, faction, or mystery actually want, in one sentence.
2. Name the bait: what does the party get offered, see, or covet that pulls them toward that want.
3. Name the obstacle: a floor, a Gate Guardian, a rival Expedition, a political wall.
4. Name the cost of success: what does winning still take from them (hours toward the Horn, reputation with someone else, an item that isn't as safe as it looks).
5. Name the seed: what does this arc's resolution plant for the next one.
Sizing an arc to a table. A one-session arc should have one obstacle and one clean payoff. A multi-session arc should have at least one moment, mid-arc, where the party's choice visibly changes the shape of the ending — otherwise it's a corridor with extra doors, not an arc.
Chapter 6 — Preparing the First Opening
The first Opening teaches the table what Decennium Descent is. Before the players know the deeper cosmology, before they understand the Gate divisions, and before they have learned to fear the clock, the first session establishes the game’s permanent rhythm: preparation, temptation, descent, consequence, and the choice to go deeper. This chapter gives you a complete procedure for preparing that beginning without requiring a finished map of all one hundred floors.
Do not begin by designing Floor 100. Begin by deciding what this Cycle means to the people standing outside Floor 1. The players should feel that the world has been waiting ten years for this moment, that thousands of lives have reorganized around it, and that their Expedition is one ambitious company among many.

Define the Starting Gate and Surface Pressure
The dungeon is the campaign’s center, but the surface determines who enters it and why. For the first Opening, prepare only the region immediately surrounding the chosen Gate. You need one authority, one market, one religious presence, one source of hirelings, and one pressure that makes delay costly even before the official clock begins.
Authority. Who controls the ground around the Gate, issues permits, settles salvage disputes, and decides whether an Expedition may establish a permanent compound?
Market. Who buys monster parts, maps, relics, and mundane salvage? What item is already scarce because thousands of delvers are purchasing it?
Faith. Which mortal interpretation of the Fifteenfold Pantheon dominates public ceremony? Remember that this religion is a mortal model, not objective cosmological truth.
Labor. Where are guides, porters, healers, cooks, translators, and guards recruited? What do they demand beyond money?
Pressure. A blockade, succession crisis, crop failure, old debt, rival nation, or public prophecy should make success this Cycle matter outside the dungeon.
The pressure should produce understandable ambition, not a mandatory plot. It explains why sensible people risk the Decennium, but it should not remove the players’ freedom to choose what kind of Expedition they become.
Choose Three Campaign Truths
Before session zero, write three statements that will remain true unless play changes them. These are not secret cosmology. They are practical campaign anchors that help you improvise consistently.
1. A public truth. Something everyone near the Gate accepts, such as “House Merrow controls legal salvage appraisal.”
2. A contested truth. Something widely argued, such as “The first Expedition through the Warded Gate always suffers a death.”
3. A hidden local truth. A secret held by a faction or NPC, such as “The authority has already sold exclusive rights to an artifact it has not yet found.”
These truths should create leverage. A fact that cannot affect a decision is background color; useful, but not part of your minimum preparation.

A rival Expedition is the easiest way to make the Opening feel competitive without turning every encounter into combat. The rival should be capable enough to matter, flawed enough to be understood, and human enough that cooperation remains possible.

Session Zero for Decennium Descent
Session zero must do more than approve characters. It establishes how the Expedition functions as an organization and how the table handles pressure, secrecy, loss, and conflict.
1. State the campaign scope. Tell the players whether you expect a one-shot, a first-push campaign, a full 2400-hour Opening, a legacy campaign, or another agreed form.
2. Explain the public Crucible myth. Calan’thir and Asmodeus forged the Divine Pact and jointly built the Crucible known to the world. That joint-creation story is the complete public explanation at campaign start. Do not mention the Heartcell, Silent Ones, Ul’nak, or Matrix of Oblivion (you hold the secret discoverer/activator layer separately — Chapter 8).
3. Teach the clock. The Opening lasts 2400 hours. The Horn sounds at hour 1200 as a cultural and strategic midpoint. The seal closes at hour 2400. The Bar of Last Refuge is a place and threshold in the depths, not another clock.
4. Define the Expedition. Confirm the twelve-character organizational cap, the five teams, and which NPC specialists may occupy seats.
5. Set table boundaries. Discuss character-versus-character conflict, theft from allies, secret bargains, romance, graphic injury, horror intensity, and what happens when a player is absent.
6. Establish death expectations. Explain the Player Rulebook’s death procedure, whether replacement characters are available, and how fallen characters are remembered by the Expedition.
7. Create the charter. Let the players decide how loot, leadership, maps, retreat calls, and recruitment are handled.
8. Choose the first objective. End session zero with one concrete reason to enter Floor 1 when the Opening begins.
The Expedition Charter
The charter is not legal decoration. It prevents the same argument from consuming table time whenever treasure is found or retreat becomes necessary. The players should write it, but you should ensure that it answers the following questions.
Who may call for a retreat, and how is the decision settled if the Combat team disagrees?
How are unique items, consumables, coin, research discoveries, and monster components divided?
Does the Expedition own maps collectively, or may individuals sell copies?
Who can recruit, dismiss, or discipline NPC specialists?
What happens to the share of a dead, missing, or sealed-in member?
Are secret side contracts allowed, and must they be disclosed after completion?
Who speaks for the Expedition to authorities, factions, and rival companies?
What conduct results in expulsion from the Expedition?
A charter may be informal, but it should exist in writing. When the players deliberately violate it, that violation becomes story: reputations shift, specialists resign, factions exploit the fracture, and rival Expeditions spread the tale.

Prepare the Opening-Day Ceremony
The Gate’s activation should feel larger than a tavern job board. Even cultures that despise one another recognize the Opening as a world-shaping event. Prepare a ceremony with five beats. Keep it short enough that the players enter the dungeon during the first session.
1. The gathering. Show the scale: rival banners, pilgrims, merchants, grieving families, opportunists, and authorities counting registered Expeditions.
2. The public myth. A speaker invokes Calan’thir, Asmodeus, the Divine Pact, or a local Fifteenfold interpretation. Different cultures may disagree about emphasis without knowing the deeper truth.
3. The warning. Name the 2400-hour limit and the consequences of being sealed inside. Let the crowd repeat the hour count.
4. The omen. Present one strange but interpretable event: a cracked bell, a silent flame, a tremor, a Guardian name whispered by no visible speaker.
5. The release. The Shardspire activates, barriers withdraw, and the first Expeditions surge forward. Ask the players what their company does while everyone else moves.
The first meaningful choice should happen before the first monster. Do they race, observe, negotiate, help an injured rival, secure hirelings, or exploit confusion? That choice announces what kind of Expedition they intend to become.

The earliest legitimate clue is usually age or architecture that does not fit accepted history—not the name of the Silent Ones or a direct explanation of the Heartcell.

Chapter 7 — Running the First Descent
The first descent is a promise. It promises that time matters, that preparation matters, that the dungeon is dangerous without being arbitrary, and that every success creates another temptation. Run it as a compressed demonstration of the full campaign loop: muster, assign, enter, observe, choose, pay, recover, and decide whether to go deeper.
Begin With the Hour
The first sentence after the Gate opens should include the current hour. “Opening hour zero” is not bookkeeping; it is the sound of the campaign beginning. State the hour again after any meaningful delay, rest, detour, or return journey. Players will learn to ask what an action costs only if you consistently tell them.
Clock discipline: Do not charge time retroactively because an outcome surprised you. State or strongly imply the likely time cost before the players commit whenever their characters could reasonably estimate it.

Before entering, ask the players to assign every available Expedition member to a team for the first period. Even a four-character Expedition should name who is taking point on Combat, Reconnaissance, Research, Resource, and Support responsibilities. Crossing the Threshold
Ask each player what their character does during the exact moment they pass from the public Gate into the Decennium. This is a compact roleplaying prompt with mechanical value. A character who marks the threshold, checks airflow, studies the wards, counts steps, or watches the crowd gives you a natural way to reveal different information.
Combat attention. Notices threat posture, choke points, and whether the nearest automaton is prepared to intervene.
Reconnaissance attention. Notices traffic patterns, alternate routes, footprints, and signs that a map is already outdated.
Research attention. Notices scripts, materials, construction seams, and details inconsistent with the public age of the Crucible.
Resource attention. Notices harvestable crystal, abandoned gear, water, fuel, and what other companies are wasting.
Support attention. Notices injured delvers, campable spaces, transport difficulties, and how far a casualty must be carried back.
This moment teaches the table that different roles reveal different versions of the same environment. It also lets you reward expertise without requiring five simultaneous dice rolls. Give automatic information first; roll only when uncertainty or pressure matters.
The Teaching Hazard
The first hazard should announce the game’s fairness standard. It must have a tell, a cost, and at least two plausible responses. A hazard that exists only to surprise and damage the party teaches caution poorly; it teaches suspicion of the Director instead.
Required feature
Example in Yieldheart Caverns
Tell
Crystalline dust trembles before a resonance pulse crosses the floor.
Cost
The pulse knocks creatures prone, cracks unsecured glass, and adds noise that attracts a patrol.
Direct response
Leap, brace, shield fragile gear, or cross between pulses.
Indirect response
Find a side shelf, dampen the crystal, follow another Expedition, or wait and spend time.
Persistent consequence
Broken gear, alerted creatures, lost hours, or a safer route now marked on the map.
When the party defeats a hazard through preparation, allow that success to matter. Do not immediately replace the avoided cost with an unrelated punishment. The dungeon is dangerous because its rules are severe, not because the Director must always collect damage.
The Teaching Fight
The first combat should reveal the difference between winning a room and surviving an expedition. Use a limited enemy roster, a readable objective, and an environment that rewards movement. Avoid introducing the campaign’s most complex conditions or recharge mechanics before the players understand their own actions.
Readable objective. Drive creatures away from a resource node, protect a wounded hireling, cross a chamber, or hold until an automaton completes a ward cycle.
One enemy role. Use skirmishers, brutes, ranged threats, or controllers—not all four in the first encounter.
One terrain rule. Shifting crystal light, unstable ledges, echo amplification, or a narrow crossing.
A retreat route. The party should know where retreat leads and what it costs.
A post-fight decision. Pursue, harvest, rescue, investigate, rest, or leave before another company arrives.
Victory does not require killing every foe. Creatures may defend territory, test intruders, feed, or respond to noise. Establishing non-suicidal monster behavior early makes later ecology believable and gives the party options beyond total extermination.

Introduce Loot as Bait Immediately
The first significant object should be useful enough to keep and complicated enough to discuss. It need not be cursed. A buyer, rumor, ownership claim, maintenance need, or strange behavior is enough to make treasure active.

The First Clue Must Be Small
A first-session clue may suggest that the public history is incomplete, but it should not explain why. Use age, materials, titles, or behavior that creates a question rather than an answer.
A foundation stone is visibly older than the Calan’thir–Asmodeus surface structure built around it.
An automaton recognizes a title such as Custodian or Signatory but ignores the names used in public hymns.
A script appears beneath a later inscription and cannot be matched to any known mortal language.
A Gate mechanism responds to conflict, sacrifice, or competing choices more strongly than to prayer.
A recovered map shows a room arranged like part of a larger machine rather than a defensive dungeon.
Do not label the clue as important. Record whether the players noticed it, what they concluded, and who else may want it. The revelation ladder works because meaning accumulates across discoveries.
The Push-or-Return Decision
End the first descent with a genuine choice between continuing and extracting. Both options should be reasonable. The party may have enough strength to proceed but possess new loot, an injured hireling, an unstable map, or information that becomes valuable only if delivered quickly.

A rival company is only minutes ahead.

Do not disguise the choice: State the likely costs clearly enough that the players understand the trade. Tension comes from competing benefits, not from hiding the fact that one option consumes twelve hours and the other consumes two.
Establishing the First Camp
If the Expedition remains inside, the first camp teaches watches, logistics, and vulnerability. A Temporary Camp is not automatically attacked. Its purpose is to create a place where choices about security, comfort, time, and location matter.
Location. Is the camp hidden, defensible, close to water, close to a route, or close to danger? It should not be all five.
Labor. Who prepares food, treats injuries, repairs gear, maps the perimeter, and stands watch?
Signature. What evidence does the camp leave—light, smoke, sound, refuse, tracks, wards, or a faction banner?
Emergency plan. Where does the Expedition retreat if the camp is compromised? Who wakes whom? What is abandoned first?
Time. State how many hours the camp activity consumes and what recovery it actually grants under the Player Rulebook.
A good camp scene should include one decision and one sign that the dungeon continues moving. Not every watch requires combat. Footprints, distant hammering, a rival signal, an altered ward, or an item behaving strangely can advance the world without interrupting rest.
Returning to the Surface
The first return should be a scene, not a bookkeeping cut. The world has changed because the dungeon opened and because the party brought something back. Give the return four beats.
1. Recognition. Who notices the Expedition first, and what assumption do they make based on injuries, missing members, or visible loot?
2. Claim. Who asserts legal, religious, contractual, familial, or criminal interest in what the party recovered?
3. Market movement. Show at least one price, rumor, or supply condition changing because of information from the depths.
4. Consequence. Resolve reputation, promises, charter shares, and the next objective before the session closes.
This return completes the campaign loop. Players should leave the first session knowing that entering the dungeon changed the surface and that the surface will change their next descent.

Handling Early Failure
The first descent can fail without ending the campaign. Failure should close one route, cost time, injure reputation, consume resources, or change who controls an objective. Avoid treating every failed check as total defeat.

Replacement Characters and Expedition Continuity
Decennium Descent is built around an Expedition larger than a single combat party. Use that structure to preserve campaign continuity after death, retirement, or player absence.
A replacement may already occupy a non-Combat Expedition seat and step forward when needed.
A faction may assign a specialist whose loyalty is divided between the party and the sponsor.
A survivor from a failed rival company may join with knowledge and unresolved obligations.
A surface contact may enter after hearing what the Expedition recovered or lost.
A character trapped on the far side of a ward may be encountered during the next push, provided the timing and fiction support it.
Do not make the new character wait several sessions for a “perfect” introduction. Bring the player back into meaningful play promptly, then let the consequences of the introduction unfold afterward.

Worked Opening: The Cycle of the Ashen Bell
The following example shows how the preparation and first-descent procedures connect. It is intentionally compact enough to adapt to another region or table.
Surface situation
The bronze bell of Saint Orra cracked during the final activation rite. Local doctrine claims the bell was cast from metal recovered during the first recorded Opening, and its fracture is treated as a warning that the Divine Pact has withdrawn favor. The city authority fears panic but cannot cancel the Opening without surrendering trade and prestige to a neighboring Gate settlement.
The five active forces
The Crystalfront Office. Issues permits and taxes verified salvage. It needs a successful Opening to preserve political legitimacy.
The Red Ledger Consortium. Has purchased debt from dozens of small Expeditions and wants exclusive access to light-bearing crystal.
The Choir of Saint Orra. Offers healing but demands that “speaking” relics be surrendered for examination.
The Brass Vow. A disciplined rival Expedition with superior logistics, weak internal trust, and a goal of reaching the Warded Gate first.
The Pale Surveyors. An old mapping fellowship operating near Floors 2–3; its members know that portions of the foundation predate accepted construction but interpret this as divine prehistory.
First objective
The party is hired to recover a survey prism lost near the Spire of Whispers. The prism is valuable because it projects remembered routes, but it has begun replaying a sound resembling the cracked bell. The Consortium wants the route data, the Choir wants the sound, and the Brass Vow needs the prism to maintain its lead.
First-session sequence
1. During the opening surge, a supply wagon overturns. Helping costs position but earns Support recruits and public goodwill.
2. At the threshold, the automatons scan the party’s banner and one addresses a character as “Bearer,” then refuses further response.
3. A resonance pulse hazard teaches timing and equipment protection.
4. Crystal-feeding scavengers occupy the prism chamber. They defend the mineral growth rather than fighting to the death.
5. The recovered prism reveals two routes: a fast unstable route toward Floor 2 and a longer route back to the surface.
6. The Brass Vow offers a temporary alliance, claiming it can interpret the prism if allowed to copy the route.
7. The party chooses whether to push, camp, negotiate, or return before the first market closes.
First reckoning possibilities
Helping the wagon delays entry by one hour but creates a pool of loyal hirelings.
Selling the prism’s route raises the party’s commercial reputation but lets rivals move faster.
Giving the bell-sound to the Choir creates sanctuary and a new religious claim on future relics.
Keeping the prism makes the Expedition a target for both purchase and theft.
Researching “Bearer” begins the first rung of the revelation ladder without naming the Heartcell.
This opening contains a public omen, a surface conflict, a rival, an active object, a teaching hazard, a teaching fight, a small lore discrepancy, and a push-or-return decision. It is enough to begin a campaign without defining the entire hundred-floor descent.
PART II — MASTER OF AETHEL'S DEPTHS
Chapter 8 — The Public Crucible and the Director's Secret
What every character in Aethel believes, and it is not a lie. After the Sundering, Calan'thir and Asmodeus forged the Divine Pact and jointly built the Crucible — also called Calan'thir's Trial — as neutral ground, a proving ground, and a monument to Order and Hell managing their differences instead of ending them by force. Cultures argue about which sovereign deserves more credit. Virtually no one disputes that both built it together. This is the complete explanation available to the world at the start of any campaign. Play it that way. It is not a cover story your players are meant to see through immediately.
🔒 Director-secret. The Silent Ones — the Old Ones called the Ul'nak after the Sundering — built the machine beneath the public Crucible: the Heartcell. It contains the hundred-floor resonance structure, the ten Gate divisions, the recurring Opening, and the reward architecture that makes loot behave as bait. Calan'thir and Asmodeus did not originate that machine. After the Old Gods were barely defeated, they found it, claimed it, modified it, activated it as a resonance valve, and bound the Divine Pact to its operation. Both histories are therefore true at different layers: the Silent Ones built the prison; Calan'thir and Asmodeus built the Crucible the world enters.
Why the machine wants loot in the world. Treasure is bait. The Heartcell needs mortals to return, choose, compete, fight, remember, and carry new ambitions back to the surface. Combat resonance, death essence, Gate victories, artifact circulation, and the stories told about them keep the machine active. Loot is not the purpose by itself; it is the invitation that continually renews difference and motion.
Why the Divine Pact keeps this quiet. The Pact is the standing agreement between Heaven and Hell that keeps the Old Ones sealed and prevents direct divine intervention from replacing mortal choice. Calan'thir and Asmodeus alone understand the full architecture; Custodians, Bearers, and Signatories may preserve fragments. The Pact's method is controlled dissonance. Conflict, ambition, memory, and choice keep resonance away from the zero-state in which the Ul'nak can complete the Matrix of Oblivion and return creation to perfect stillness.
The Matrix of Oblivion. Use this chain as the Director's simplest model of what the dungeon preserves:
Possibility → Choice → Difference → Conflict → Motion
→ Time → History → Identity → Will → Life

Oblivion seeks P = C = D = K = M = T = H = I = W = L = 0
When distinction reaches zero, Resonance reaches zero; the Weave collapses into stillness.
The Heartcell as living firewall. Every descent forces possibility into choice, choice into difference, and difference into motion. Gate victories, losses, rivalries, discoveries, and artifacts keep the chain from collapsing. The Heartcell is therefore both prison and resonance valve: a machine built by the Silent Ones and repurposed by the Divine Pact to keep their zero-state from consuming Aethel.
The revelation ladder — how a party climbs toward this, if they ever do. The wider world never learns any of this. A sufficiently successful, sufficiently curious Expedition might, one rung at a time:
1. They notice architecture on deep floors that predates any written script or founding myth they can find.
2. They find records — a diary, a seal, a title carved into stone — referring to figures as Custodians, Bearers, or Signatories, never once naming Calan'thir or Asmodeus directly.
3. They realize the Pact governs the dungeon's rules but did not originate them — a subtle but load-bearing distinction once they see it stated plainly somewhere.
4. They make direct contact with the Heartcell's operating cycle itself — at which point they know, and the question becomes what they do with it.
Pace this ladder across a whole campaign, not a single dungeon. Never let one lucky roll skip more than one rung. And when a party does reach rung four, remember that the world outside still doesn't know — their discovery changes their picture of Aethel, not everyone else's.
What this means for your prose. Keep public NPCs, temple doctrine, and newcomer-facing material entirely at the level of the public myth. When you write Gate Guardian lore, faction doctrine, or haven rumor for players, it should never leak the Heartcell or Silent Ones by name — clues should feel like loose threads, not footnotes.
The Fifteenfold Pantheon and the Great Wheel. These are mortal religious models, not objective cosmic truth. Priests, scholars, and nations may believe them sincerely, and their faith can still produce real social and magical consequences. Director-secret hierarchy places the Spiral or Source first, then the Old Ones/Ul'nak and Heartcell, then Calan'thir and Asmodeus, then the First Choir, with the Fifteenfold and later cultural gods as mortal attempts to describe what lies beyond them.
Chapter 9 — Staging the Decennium: Vertical Design
The Decennium is a hundred floors organized into ten Gate divisions of ten floors each. Treat vertical design as your primary map-making tool — you are not drawing a dungeon so much as staging a descent with ten distinct acts.
Design principles for a tier.
One dominant sensory idea. Every tier gazetteer entry in Chapter 10 leans on one strong image (crystalline caverns, bioluminescent marsh, molten forge, sun-drenched archipelago, cathedral of sound). Borrow this discipline for any floor you invent: pick one idea and let every room reinforce it.
A patrol or hazard gradient. Early tiers (1–3) have some external safety net — automaton sentinels, established haven routes. That safety net should visibly end at a specific gate or threshold, and everything past it should feel unsupervised.
A gate, not a door. Each tier ends at a named Gate with its own unlock condition — a puzzle, a ritual, a sacrifice of resources, a fight. The Gate should require something the Expedition had to earn on that tier, not an object they could have brought from the surface.
A named guardian. Every Gate has a Guardian (Chapter 11). Its presence should be felt in tier rumor and environmental storytelling well before the party meets it.
A reason to turn back that isn't a wall. The best tiers give a party a genuine, in-fiction reason to reconsider going deeper — dwindling supplies, an omen, a returning survivor's warning — rather than relying purely on monster difficulty to gatekeep depth.
The civilization line. Tiers 1 through 6 can support small civilizations, havens, and floor cultures. Tier 6 is the last known depth where stable settlement remains possible. Tiers 7 through 10 are the wild unknown: maps decay, supply lines fail, and survivor testimony becomes fragmentary. Treat those deeper tiers as controlled mystery, not empty space.
The Bar of Last Refuge. Most Expeditions that reach Tier 5's Sanctum of Echoing Choirs turn back there because gear, nerve, and Opening hours have already been consumed. The Bar is a cultural threshold and pacing landmark, not the Horn itself. Depending on when the party arrived, the temporal point of no return may have already sounded or may still be approaching.
Staging a Gate Guardian encounter as a set piece, not a wall. Build the room around three moments: an approach that telegraphs the Guardian's theme (sound in the Sanctum, oath-weight in the Pactbound Arena, cooling reflection in the Duskshard Pools), a confrontation with at least one mechanical hook unique to that Guardian, and an aftermath that changes something durable — a floor unlocked, a title earned, a wound that follows the party home.
🔒 Director-secret — the Old God Interstice. Between Tier 7 and Tier 8, after Xarog and before Eirfax, lies the established Old God encounter. It is not an eleventh tier, not a Gate Guardian, and not ordinary floor content. Treat it as a campaign-scale threshold that exposes the prison beneath the Crucible and tests whether the party is ready for the Matrix revelation.
Chapter 10 — The Tier Gazetteer
Use bestiary names for every Gate Guardian listed here — the older cosmology-only god-names for these roles (certain archaic combat-figure names once used for gate bosses) are formally retired wherever they would conflict with this roster. This is the only canon roster for play.
Tier 1 — Yieldheart Caverns (Floors 1–10) · Guardian: Vaskor, the Stone Maw
The winding, crystalline gateway into the depths. Calcified stalactites drip with luminescent mineral veins, throwing fractured light across slick floors. Automaton sentinels — ancient constructs bound to the Pact's oldest wards — patrol the first three floors and turn back anyone plainly unprepared; past Floor 3, the machines do not follow, and every echoing footstep is a reminder that the safety net has ended.
Spire of Whispers (Floor 1): A towering stalagmite that hums when the wind shifts underground. Some swear the murmur carries warning; others call it prophecy.
Luminous Veins (Floors 1–2): Phosphorescent ore seams thread the walls. Harvested carefully, a shard lights a torch all night — but chipping at the cavern's own backbone rarely goes unnoticed for long.
Echoing Gallery (Floor 2): A chamber that magnifies every sound tenfold. A careless shout here can wake something dormant, or panic your own Expedition.
Abandoned Stoneworks (Floor 3): Ruined forges and rusted tools mark an old dwarven attempt to tame these caves. Salvageable, if the hidden traps don't get there first.
Warded Gate (end of Floor 3): A rune-carved arch sealing Floors 4–10 behind a hidden glyph puzzle. Failure invites arcane backlash; success ends the automatons' reach for good.
Tier 2 — Bloomdeep Marsh (Floors 11–20) · Guardian: The Bloom That Hungers
A self-contained, waterlogged realm of glowing fungus, tangled reeds, and mist-shrouded pools — no automaton patrol has ever reached this deep, and no outside supply line does either. Survival here means learning the marsh's rhythm or finding one of its hidden refuges.
Reedcaller's Circle (Floor 11): A natural amphitheater of towering rushes where even a whisper carries for dozens of yards — useful for signaling, deadly if the wrong ears are listening.
Spore-Veil Falls (Floor 12): A cascade of sap that bursts into blinding spores on impact; timing a dash between spurts is a test of nerve.
Phantom Mire (Floors 12–13 border): A deceptively shallow, ghost-lit pool. A misstep swallows you whole, and the light guides worse things to the struggle.
Overgrown Reliquary (Floors 14–15): Moss-shrouded statues of long-dead explorers ring a cracked sarcophagus; the tools inside are still serviceable, if the animated root-graspers allow it.
Sporemist Gate (end of Floor 20): A blackened iron arch strangled in vines. Burning three Heartroot Blossoms at its brazier cleanses the wards; failing floods the chamber with necrotic backlash. Beyond it waits the Bloom That Hungers, and Tier 3.
Tier 3 — Anvil of Brogi (Floors 21–30) · Guardian: Ashthirst the Hollow
Brogi's primordial forge: molten rivers, echoing hammer-strikes, towering pillars of smelted obsidian. Heat and sulfur shimmer the air; molten droplets fall like living coals. As deep as the automatons never went, and hotter than most Expeditions are prepared for.
Hammerfall Hall (Floor 21): Pillars drip molten metal from the ceiling; every tremor sends glowing slag across the floor, and timing your steps is a matter of life or death.
Molten River Crossing (Floor 22): A braided stream of liquid iron beneath rusted dwarven handrails; some sections run white-hot even fire-walkers hesitate to cross.
Chained Anvil Chamber (Floor 24): A gargantuan hammer and anvil swing on infernal chains in slow arcs, touching the floor only once every few minutes — and crushing whatever waits there.
Rivetworks Maze (Floor 26): Half-finished girders and spiked rivets shift subtly, as if the labyrinth is alive, forcing constant recalibration or a skewering.
Lavaflow Gate (end of Floor 30): A smoldering iron arch. A key must be forged by plunging a cooled slag shard into Brogi's Quenching Pool; failure vents superheated steam and molten metal.
Tier 4 — Isles of Eternal Radiance (Floors 31–40) · Guardian: Gor Vekk, Architect of Ends
A self-contained archipelago bathed permanently in dawn's first light. Floating islets of sun-kissed stone drift over a sea of glowing mist, joined by translucent crystal bridges. Radiant elementals and light-forged constructs replace the automatons of earlier tiers — only those who can harness or withstand brilliance itself endure here.
Prismatic Archipelago (Floor 31): Six islets crowned with refractive sunstone; crystal bridges shift angle constantly, creating moving paths — and deadly gaps for anyone who hesitates.
Radiant Bloom Gardens (Floor 32): Floating terraces of dawn-glow flowers whose petals brew into a temporary resistance to radiant harm, if harvested past the patrolling light-sprites.
Mirror Reef (Floor 34): A liquid-crystal lagoon so reflective it spawns illusory doubles of anything that disturbs its surface — some of which rise from the water uninvited.
Sunforged Obelisk (Floor 36): A thirty-foot sunstone monolith with rotating glyph-rings. Aligning a light-shaft to strike its apex opens a hidden grotto of heat-shields and sun-steel blades.
Radiance Gate (end of Floor 40): A golden quartz archway with three empty sun-shard recesses. Filling them with charged vials at dawn's exact zenith opens the way; failure floods the chamber in blinding radiance.
Tier 5 — Sanctum of Echoing Choirs (Floors 41–50) · Guardian: Choralith, Voice Below
A vast, vaulted cathedral of living sound, where every whisper swells into symphony — and where the overwhelming majority of expeditions in Aethel's history end. Time bleeds away here mending gear and nerve; automated patrols are long gone, and the closer the party draws to Choralith's domain, the more every echo frays their composure even as a strange, dangerous calm settles in. The Bar of Last Refuge marks the last place most veterans still consider retreat sensible; it is not the Horn, which is measured by the Opening clock.
Whispering Pillars (Floor 41): Twelve columns that capture and replay the faintest sound; veterans describe undercurrents that feel almost like other minds brushing theirs.
Aeolian Mirror Pools (Floor 42): Still silver basins that reflect both light and tone; a dropped pebble twists rippling arias into something that might, almost, answer back.
Resonant Reliquary (Floor 44): Crystal ossuaries holding preserved echoes of ancient choirs. Opening one can calm or fracture a mind — and may wake hidden tone-wardens.
A Cappella Alcove (Floor 46): A gallery where machinery falls silent and only the unamplified voice carries. One misstep in sound, or in stillness, triggers concealed sentinel statues.
Canticle Gate (end of Floor 50): An obsidian arch set with ten tuning-fork glyphs. Four notes drawn from the Whispering Pillars, struck in perfect sequence, unlock it; failure unleashes a backlash that rends armor and spirit alike.
Tier 6 — Crossroads of the Shattered Thread (Floors 51–60) · Guardian: Lumen of the Shattered Thread
The Crossroads is the final tier known to support small floor civilizations and stable havens. Beyond the Canticle Gate, broken Weave-threads tangle into a labyrinth of half-real pathways, but isolated communities have learned to anchor a few routes long enough to survive. Past the Lumen Gate, stable civilization ends.
Shattered Crossroads (Floor 51): The tier's central hazard. Footprints fade where paths meet, leaving only the pull of a route that may not exist when the party turns back.
Lumen Gate (end of Floor 60): Its threshold glows brightest where threads gather most tightly, offering no clue which way the light intends. Defeat unlocks Tier 7.
Tier 7 — Pactbound Arena (Floors 61–70) · Guardian: Xarog, the Pactbound Furnace
Survivor-rumor stub — expand at your table. A crucible of bargains and bloodsport, where power is bought with oaths. The stone here carries the weight of promises made before any current Expedition arrived; few maps survive long enough to explain what was paid for passage.
Pactbound Coliseum (Floor 61): The tier's defining hazard — every empty seat feels occupied by the memory of an oath, and the floor waits with the patience of an unwritten contract.
Furnace Gate (end of Floor 70): Heat pools behind sealed ironwork, as if listening for the last word of a bargain no survivor can quote in full.
Interstice — The Old God (between Tiers 7 and 8)
🔒 Director-secret. No public map records this place as Floor 70 or Floor 71. The Furnace Gate can expose the route, but defeating Xarog does not automatically force the confrontation. The Old God is a campaign-scale presence whose awakening, prison, or bargain should permanently change how the Expedition understands the Decennium.
Tier 8 — Duskshard Pools (Floors 71–80) · Guardian: Eirfax, the Dusk Crowned
Survivor-rumor stub — expand at your table. Twilight basins where light and hush trade places, tasting of cooling metal and old prayers. A reflection lingers a moment after the lantern that cast it has moved on.
Duskshard Basins (Floor 71): A surface that looks still from one bank can show a different dusk from the next — every crossing a quiet test of nerve.
Crown Gate (end of Floor 80): Rises from the deepest reflected light, its crown-shaped silhouette clearer in the water than in the air above it.
Tier 9 — Veiled Iron Spiral (Floors 81–90) · Guardian: Threxus, the Iron Spiral
Survivor-rumor stub — expand at your table. A descending helix of iron and shadow. Each coil tightens the Weave around those who refuse to turn back, hiding both the path ahead and the last bend behind.
Veiled Helix (Floor 81): Distance itself feels like something the Spiral withholds from anyone who has come this far.
Spiral Gate (end of Floor 90): Another turn of the same iron made absolute, its seam visible only when shadow slips across it.
Tier 10 — Silencebound Void (Floors 91–100) · Guardian: The Marquis of Silence
Survivor-rumor stub — expand at your table. The hush beneath the song. The Marquis waits where even the First Choir's echo fades, and the Void presses close enough that a party's unspoken questions begin to feel louder than their voices.
Hush Threshold (Floor 91): No clean boundary — sound simply thins until the party notices the last reply never arrived.
Marquis Gate (end of Floor 100): The Decennium's deepest gate, waiting without ceremony, its stillness making every story of the depths feel unfinished.
Chapter 11 — Gate Guardians and How to Stage Seals
The canonical Gate Guardian roster: Vaskor the Stone Maw · The Bloom That Hungers · Ashthirst the Hollow · Gor Vekk, Architect of Ends · Choralith, Voice Below · Lumen of the Shattered Thread · Xarog, the Pactbound Furnace · Eirfax, the Dusk Crowned · Threxus, the Iron Spiral · The Marquis of Silence. The Old God between Tiers 7 and 8 is not a Gate Guardian and does not replace any name on this list. Full stat blocks, ecology, and lair notes belong to the Codex of the Heartcell; this chapter teaches you how to stage a seal encounter.
A Guardian is a seal, not a random boss. Every Guardian stands at the seam between tiers and should feel like a rule of the dungeon made physical. Vaskor embodies the end of the automaton safety net; Choralith embodies the Sanctum's fraying calm. Spend as much preparation on what the confrontation means as on its numbers.
Three-beat staging.
1. Telegraph. The tier's rumor and environment should mention the Guardian well before the fight — a name half-remembered by a returning survivor, an environmental detail that echoes its theme.
2. Confrontation. Give every Guardian at least one mechanical hook nothing else on the tier does — a Gate-specific hazard, an environmental interaction, a phase change tied to the room rather than a generic health-percentage trigger.
3. Aftermath. Victory should change something durable: the tier's Gate opens, a title or faction reputation shifts, or the party carries a mark (a scar, a haunting, a piece of the Guardian's domain) into the next tier.
Playtest honesty at the table. When a Guardian or bestiary entry is still being balanced, say so before the encounter. Explain that hit points, recharge timing, or one ability may be adjusted only if the draft is clearly miscalibrated. Do not disguise development uncertainty as secret difficulty.
Recharge and unique abilities. A Guardian's signature recharge chance must come from its current stat block. Roll recharge openly unless a specific ability says otherwise, and track the result at the table.
Chapter 12 — Factions, Settlements, and Reputation
Reputation as a lever, not a scoreboard. Every faction relationship runs on a five-step ladder: Hated → Disliked → Neutral → Liked → Honored. A tracker may store an underlying score, but at the table name the relationship tier. "The Arcane Guilds consider you Honored" lands harder than "plus two."
The four Resonance-linked tracks — Academic Factions, Religious Orders, Arcane Guilds, Nature Circles — are canon and mechanically wired: Arcane Guilds standing shifts a Weave caster's maximum Resonance Points; the class-relevant track shifts spell cost by ±1; Nature Circles standing shifts Retune recovery. Treat these four as your primary dial for "the world noticing what this caster has been doing" between sessions.
Social and background factions (Aethel Authorities, Merchant Guilds, Dark Cults, and others drawn from character backgrounds) run on the same ladder without a direct Resonance hook — their leverage is entirely narrative: doors opened or closed, prices adjusted, informants offered or withheld.
Creating a faction or settlement. Start from want, not from lore density. Ask: what does this group need that an Expedition can provide (or threaten), and what does it control that an Expedition wants? A haven trading post wants steady salvage and fears bandit or monster incursion from a nearby floor mouth. A temple order wants relics returned intact and fears heretical misuse of dungeon-touched power. A rival Expedition's backers want exclusivity on a route and fear a rival's success more than they fear the dungeon itself. Two or three of these wants, stated plainly, are worth more prep time than a full family tree of NPCs nobody will meet.
Settlements at Gate seams. Havens tend to cluster just above or just below a Gate — they exist because Expeditions need somewhere to resupply, sell salvage, and recover between pushes. A settlement's character should reflect its tier: a Tier 1 haven trades in caution and salvage-appraisal; a Tier 5 haven, this close to the Bar of Last Refuge, trades in grim professionalism and very expensive last-minute gear.
Reputation consequences, worked example. A party publicly humiliates a Merchant Guild representative while fencing suspicious loot (Disliked → Hated). Next Opening, prices at that Guild's stalls rise, their informants dry up, and a rival Expedition — backed by that same Guild — gets first pick of a contested haven contract. None of this requires new mechanics; it requires you writing the consequence down and paying it off on schedule.
Chapter 13 — Creating NPCs
Not every NPC needs the same weight. Decide a tier of detail before you invent anything else.
Tier 1 — Set dressing. A name, one physical detail, one verbal tic. Exists to make a scene feel populated. Never needs stats.
Tier 2 — Functional contact. A name, a want, a faction affiliation, and one piece of information or one service they provide. Most haven vendors, guides, and quest-givers live here. Give them stats only if a fight becomes plausible.
Tier 3 — Recurring ally or rival. Everything in Tier 2, plus a relationship arc: how their opinion of the party can move (tie this explicitly to the Reputation ladder in Chapter 12), and one secret or complication that can surface later. NPC specialists recruited into an Expedition's Support or Resource seats (Chapter 4) usually belong here.
Tier 4 — Villain or patron. Everything in Tier 3, plus a clear want stated in Chapter 5's terms, a resource or leverage that makes them dangerous even without combat stats, and — critically — a reason their goal is legible dissonance: they want conflict kept alive, a grudge unresolved, a rival Expedition ruined, or a secret kept buried past its safe date. The best patrons and villains in this setting are compelling precisely because their motives make uncomfortable sense once you notice the Pact rewards exactly that kind of appetite.
Umbra and Pact-adjacent contacts. Some NPCs — a temple official who quietly serves infernal interests, an Umbra-aligned broker, a Custodian's estranged descendant — sit closer to Chapter 8's secret stratum than most. These NPCs should never state Director-secret cosmology outright; they gesture at it, contradict the public myth in small ways, or possess a fragment of the revelation ladder without understanding its full shape. Use them as your primary delivery mechanism for that ladder — a person is a better vehicle for a slow-burn revelation than a wall inscription.

Combat stats for NPCs. Do not build a full stat block for a Tier 1 or Tier 2 NPC unless a fight is actually about to happen. When it is, reuse the closest appropriate bestiary humanoid entry and reskin the name and a flavor ability rather than authoring new numbers from scratch — this is both faster and more consistent with the rest of your table's expectations.
PART III — MASTER OF THE WEAVE
Chapter 14 — Adjudicating Resonance
The one rule you must enforce from session zero: Resonance is a pool, not slots. Every Weave caster carries one fungible pool of Resonance Points (RP). There is no "first-level slot" and "second-level slot" language anywhere in this system — a caster spends RP from a single bar on whatever prepared spell they choose. If a player at your table says "I have a 2nd-level slot left," gently correct them: they have RP left, and RP does not care what "level" it came from.
Pool size. Use the class tables in the Player Rulebook. Wizard, Mage, Cleric, and Druid have complete level 1–20 Resonance tables. When a Weave-casting class lacks a finalized table, identify the fallback as playtest material and write the chosen progression on the character sheet before play begins; never change the curve mid-session.
Casting cost.
Cost = (spell level × 2)
     − 1  if the spell's school is in key with the caster's alignment (affinity)
     + 1  if the school is dissonant against the caster's alignment (opposition)
     ± class-relevant faction reputation modifier (Chapter 12)
     − specialization discount (0 to −3, when granted by a class feature)
→ final cost floors at 0
Cantrips always cost 0 RP.
Worked example — a Cleric at level 3 (max 7 RP). Casting Cure Wounds at 1st level with a Transmutation affinity: 1 × 2 = 2, minus 1 for affinity, costs 1 RP. Casting Spiritual Weapon at 2nd level with no affinity or opposition: 2 × 2 = 4 RP, dropping the Cleric to 2 of 7 — right at the Burnout threshold (below).
Recovery. A short rest grants Retune: recover one quarter of maximum RP, plus the spellcasting ability modifier and any applicable Nature Circles benefit. A long rest grants Full Harmony and restores the pool completely; it also consumes eight hours of the Opening. A once-per-day ten-minute Meditation restores RP equal to the spellcasting ability modifier when that option is in use.
Overcast and Burnout. Extended, Subtle, and Distant each cost +1 RP; Empowered costs +2; Widened, Quickened, and Twinned cost +3; Heightened costs +4. Burnout risk begins when the remaining pool falls below one quarter of maximum after a cast. Until a final Burnout table is published, use a clearly announced playtest consequence — disadvantage on Weave-related checks until the end of the caster's next turn — and record how it affected play.
Multiclass Resonance. The Player Rulebook still contains legacy multiclass language written in spell-slot terms. Do not use that passage for a Resonance character. Until the Resonance multiclass table is finalized, use this explicit playtest fallback: full RP from the highest-progression Weave class, plus 25% of the secondary class's RP, rounded up; further Weave classes add no additional pool. Apply reputation modifiers once to the merged total.
Oracle is not a Weave caster. Oracle spends Revelation Charges (RC), starting at 3 and capping at 7, gaining one at every odd level. Revelations cost 1, 2, or 3 charges by tier. Charges recover through the Mystery's Prophecy Trigger, not through ordinary rest. Do not grant a short-rest charge unless a specific Revelation, Curse, or campaign effect says so.
The one sentence to repeat at every session zero: "You have one Resonance pool. Cantrips are free. Resting partially refills it. Oracle doesn't use this system at all — Oracle has its own charges that come back from doing Oracle things, not from sleeping."
Chapter 15 — Combat as Expected Value
You do not need a perfect encounter-math system to run fair fights — you need a habit of estimating pressure before the dice hit the table, and adjusting on the fly when your estimate was wrong.
The core estimate.
Rounds to resolve ≈ foe total hit points ÷ party damage per round
Incoming pressure ≈ (foe damage per round × number of foes) × rounds to resolve
Compare incoming pressure to the party's total hit points. If incoming pressure clears more than roughly half the party's total HP before the fight likely ends, expect a hard fight — brief it as such if you want to, or pull a punch quietly if the table isn't looking for that kind of night. If incoming pressure is a small fraction of party HP, the fight is a formality — consider whether it needs to happen mechanically at all, or whether Quick Resolution (Chapter 2) serves the scene better.
Adjust for what raw math misses. Terrain that limits how many foes can engage at once effectively lowers incoming pressure per round. Action denial — a stun, a restrain, a forced retreat — removes a foe's damage for that round entirely; account for it rather than re-deriving the whole estimate. A Support team waiting at camp (Chapter 4) effectively adds hit points to the party's pool if retreat and treatment are both realistic options this scene.
Validate the source numbers. Encounter estimates are only as good as the stat blocks used. Check AC, hit points, attacks, and recharge text before relying on the calculation, especially for provisional creatures. For finalized Gate Guardians, use the current published stat block rather than an older campaign note.
When your estimate was wrong, mid-fight. Don't silently fudge dice. Instead, reach for legitimate levers: reinforcements arrive late or not at all, a foe breaks morale and flees, an environmental hazard the party can exploit becomes obvious, or (rarely, and told to the table afterward) you quietly adjust a monster's remaining hit points because the sandbox number was clearly miscalibrated. Owning the adjustment out loud, after the fact, keeps trust intact.
Chapter 16 — Mass Combat and the Eight-Actor Rule
Full Expeditions, coalition battles, and haven sieges can put far more than a standard party's worth of combatants on the field. Trying to run twenty individual initiative slots in detailed turn order is the single fastest way to stall a table for an hour on one fight.
The rule: keep at most about eight creatures acting in detailed initiative at once. Everything else on the field is abstracted.
How to abstract the rest.
Reinforcement clocks. A wave of additional foes arrives in N rounds unless the party interrupts the trigger (destroying a summoning point, winning a parallel Recon check, closing a gate). Track this as a simple countdown, not as combatants waiting off-stage in your notes.
Morale tracks. A mass of lesser foes breaks and flees once a threshold is crossed (their leader falls, half their number is down, a terrifying display lands) — resolve this as a single check against the whole group rather than tracking each one's morale individually.
Off-map objectives. A second front — Recon holding a chokepoint, Support defending the camp, an allied unit trading blows with a mirror force — resolves through a handful of periodic checks (once per few rounds) rather than full turn-by-turn play. Narrate the outcome of each check vividly; the table should feel the stakes without tracking the mechanics.
Worked example — a haven under siege. The players' Combat team (4 detailed actors) fights alongside two haven guards (2 more detailed actors) at the gate — six total, comfortably under the cap. Meanwhile: a reinforcement clock ticks down for a second raider wave (3 rounds unless the Recon team, resolved separately, disables the signal fire); a morale track governs the raider mass at the wall (breaks if their captain, one of the six detailed actors, falls); and an off-map objective tracks whether the Support team's triage keeps pace with the wounded (one check every two rounds). The table experiences an epic siege without anyone tracking twenty stat blocks in turn order.
When to promote an abstracted element to a detailed actor. If the players make a plan specifically targeting an off-map or abstracted element (sneaking around to sabotage the reinforcement trigger personally, for instance), bring the relevant piece into detailed play for that scene. Abstraction is a pacing tool, not a permanent wall between the party and the wider battle.
Chapter 17 — Creating Monsters, Quickly
Most of the time, you do not need to open a spreadsheet mid-session. This chapter is the fast path; the Codex of the Heartcell is where you go for full ecology, lair actions, and legendary-scale design.
The quick method.
1. Pick the closest existing bestiary entry by rough power level and reskin its name, description, and one signature ability. This is faster and more balance-consistent than inventing from nothing.
2. If nothing fits, scale from a template. Take a same-tier creature's AC and HP as your baseline, give it one to three attacks appropriate to its concept, and add exactly one memorable trait — a recharge ability, an environmental interaction, or a condition it inflicts. Resist giving a quick monster more than one standout trick; that's what makes Gate Guardians feel special by comparison.
3. State its role before its stats. Is it a single hard-hitting threat, a swarm meant to be handled with area effects, a controller meant to lock down one PC, or a hazard-with-a-face meant to be avoided rather than fought? Decide this first; it tells you which numbers actually matter.
Recording reusable creatures. If you invent a creature that will recur — a new floor culture enemy, recurring rival, or tier-defining monster — add it to the campaign's working bestiary with a version date and playtest note. Reusable content should not remain trapped in one night's scratch paper.
Labeling. Anything built during play is a provisional reskin or playtest creature until reviewed. Say so when the distinction matters, and never present an improvised number as settled canon.
Chapter 18 — Treasure, Loot-as-Bait, and Magic Item Creation
Every piece of treasure is doing a job for the machine, whether your players know it or not (Chapter 8). Design loot with that job in mind: an item should create at least one interesting complication for the party that carries it, not just a bigger number on a character sheet.
A quick framework for any dungeon-touched item.
What it does (the obvious benefit):
What it costs (a complication, a hunger, a rumor it attracts, a faction that wants it back):
Who else wants it, and why:
What happens if it's ever destroyed, sold, or returned to the depths:
An item that only has the first line is a stat bonus. An item that has all four is a hook generator that will feed your campaign for sessions.
Worked example. A sun-steel blade from the Radiance Gate's grotto (Tier 4). Obvious benefit: deals extra radiant damage and shrugs off cold. Cost: it hungers for direct light and dims noticeably — and its wielder grows uneasy — after more than a day away from sun or fire. Who wants it: a Tier 4 haven forge-guild that lost the original casting formula and would pay well, or trade dangerously, to study it. What happens if returned to the depths: the Gate's grotto restocks its recesses slightly faster next Cycle — a detail only a Director-secret-aware party would ever connect to anything.
Treasure and awards. The cumulative level 1–20 XP thresholds in the Player Rulebook are canon. Specific encounter and objective awards remain Director judgments unless a published adventure provides them. Use milestones when they fit the campaign better, but tell the table which advancement method is in force before the Opening begins.
Research unlocks as treasure. Not every reward needs to be an object. A Research team's translated inscription that reveals a Gate's true unlock condition, a map fragment that saves an entire tier's worth of wandering, or a faction's grudging respect are all valid "treasure" — and they tie the reward economy directly back to the Expedition structure in Chapter 4 instead of only to Combat's loot rolls.
Chapter 19 — Craft and Research as Machine-Feed
Crafting and research are not side activities bolted onto combat — they are how an Expedition converts what it survives into what it can do next, and (per Chapter 8) they are one more way the machine keeps mortals coming back.
Craft. Recipes draw components from bestiary loot tiers, so a Resource team's hunting choices should visibly feed later crafting. Where a published recipe's component list is incomplete, assign a finite and reasonable salvage requirement; do not treat missing text as permission for free or unlimited crafting.
Research. Research teams translate, identify, and map. Spire lore, Gate-rune inscriptions, and Surveyor seals all function as a kind of expedition currency — spend them like treasure, not like flavor text. Between-session research (Chapter 24) is the right tool for anything that would otherwise burn table time on repeated checks with no dramatic content.
The machine-feed framing. Every crafted weapon carried back into the depths, every translated Gate-rune that opens a seal faster next time, and every research chain that identifies a dungeon-touched item well enough to sell it are all, in the end, more resonance flowing through the Heartcell (Chapter 8). You never need to say this to your players — but keeping it in your head as you design craft and research content will naturally make those systems feel connected to the setting's deeper logic instead of feeling like a detached minigame.
Chapter 20 — Hazards, Watches, and Logistics
Watch rotations fail — plan for it. A watch schedule that never gets interrupted is a watch schedule that isn't doing dramatic work. Build in the possibility (not the certainty) that a watch fails: exhaustion, a Support team stretched too thin, a hazard specifically suited to bypass a sentry.
Environmental attrition by tier. Sun-shields and radiant-resistance items deplete in Tier 4's Isles of Eternal Radiance. Spore and heat exposure punishes an unprepared Resource team in Tiers 2 and 3. Sound discipline matters constantly from Tier 5 onward. Match your hazard pressure to the tier's dominant sensory idea (Chapter 9) rather than reusing generic trap tables everywhere.
Rations, tool quality, and triage. Track these loosely rather than to the exact ounce — the point is narrative pressure, not bookkeeping. A Support team's quality of triage should visibly matter: a well-equipped, well-staffed Support seat should mean wounded characters recover faster and injuries scar less severely than a threadbare one.
Who decides who stands. Give your healers real authority in a crisis — a Choir-Covenant-style convention where the party's primary healer has the final call on who gets treated first when everyone is arguing at once — prevents the same argument from eating table time in every hard fight. Establish this convention at session zero, not mid-crisis.
Forward camps as operational centers. A Forward Base (Chapter 4) should function at the table as a genuine safe harbor: a place to rest, restock, repair, and plan without narrating every minute of downtime. Treat time spent productively at a good base camp as one of the more valuable things an Expedition can invest in, not a delay from the "real" adventure.
Chapter 21 — Rules Adjudication Extras
Some situations come up often enough at a Decennium Descent table that they deserve a standing house ruling rather than an improvised one every time.
Chases through a tier. A chase across a dungeon floor should feel different depending on the tier's dominant terrain. Resolve it as a short series of opposed checks (appropriate skill vs. appropriate skill, or a straight ability contest), with each exchange narrated through that tier's signature hazard — a chase through Bloomdeep Marsh should involve at least one Phantom-Mire-style false step; a chase through the Anvil of Brogi should threaten at least one molten-crossing moment. Three successful exchanges (for either side) usually resolves a chase cleanly; adjust up for a chase you want to matter more.
Sieges of an Expedition's camp. Treat a camp siege as mass combat (Chapter 16) with the camp's defenses (palisade, ward-lines, hired guards) acting as a standing detailed actor or a morale-track modifier rather than as terrain alone. A Fortified Outpost should measurably outperform a Temporary Camp under siege — if it doesn't, your camp-tier mechanics aren't doing their job.
Unusual situations, a general method. When a player asks for something the rulebook doesn't explicitly cover, resolve it in three steps: name the closest existing mechanic (a skill check, a saving throw, an EV estimate), set a difficulty or stake that matches the fiction's risk, and — if it's a big enough moment — let the outcome create a consequence that persists (Chapter 2's "the reckoning"). Consistency across sessions matters more than any single ruling being perfectly optimized.
Falling, environmental hazards, and other single-check situations. Default to the simplest fair resolution: an appropriate save or check, with damage or a condition scaled to the fictional severity, rather than inventing bespoke sub-systems for every environmental hazard. Save your bespoke-mechanic effort for hazards that recur often enough to be worth teaching the table (a tier's signature threat), not one-off flavor.
When a ruling interacts with a class limit. Let the most specific published class feature, action-economy limit, copy limit, or aura limit control for that session. Record any ambiguity and clarify it before the next session instead of relitigating it during a player's turn.
Chapter 22 — Creating Content That Serves Dissonance
Everything you invent for this game — a floor, a faction, a villain, an item — should be tested against one question: does this create difference, choice, and change, or does it quietly resolve toward stillness?
Avoid a true peace as an end state, unless you mean it. Per Chapter 8, absolute peace in Aethel is not a cozy epilogue — it's the trigger condition for the Old Ones waking to silence the universe again. A campaign that drives toward "everyone finally gets along forever" should either knowingly be building toward that specific apocalypse, or should be redirected toward a managed, ongoing tension instead — a truce, not an ending.
Favor hooks that force more than murder. A hook that only rewards killing something is thinner than a hook that also forces research, craft, political fallout, or a hard choice about a faction relationship. This isn't a moral stance about combat — it's a design habit that naturally uses more of the Expedition structure (Chapter 4) and keeps non-Combat players engaged.
Design villains and mysteries that rhyme with the machine, without stating it. The best original antagonists in this setting want dissonance kept alive for their own reasons that happen to serve the Pact's actual function, whether they know it or not. A temple zealot who provokes unnecessary wars "to test the faithful," a merchant who profits specifically from prolonged Expedition rivalries, a Custodian-descended noble who suppresses evidence that would calm two warring factions — none of these need to know about the Heartcell to function as excellent, thematically resonant content.
A closing design checklist for new content.
Does it introduce a real choice, not just a fight?
Does it create at least one lasting consequence either way?
Does it avoid resolving toward total peace or total stillness?
Does it keep Director-secret cosmology out of player-facing text?
Does it use bestiary/Gate names, not retired or foreign cosmology?
PART IV — RUNNING THE TABLE
Chapter 23 — Problem Players and Pacing
The novice-caster confusion. By far the most common friction at a new table is a player calling Resonance Points "spell slots," expecting full recovery on a short rest, or forgetting Oracle doesn't use RP at all. Correct it gently, every time, without making the player feel singled out — this genuinely takes several sessions to fully unlearn for anyone coming from a slot-based background, and that's normal.
The support player who heals the wrong target. A near-universal early mistake: healing a healthy ally while another character sits at zero hit points. Before accepting any heal in a tense moment, ask the table out loud, "who is actually dying right now?" — this single habit prevents most of the worst version of this mistake.
The optimizer pushing a class ceiling cap. When a player tries to chain two of the same high-impact action in one round (a second mutation activation, a second Revelation, a second smite), point to the relevant draft ceiling cap (Chapter 21) plainly rather than relitigating it as a surprise "no." Players respect a documented limit far more than an improvised one that appears only when they try to break it.
The player who checks out during non-combat spotlight. If a table has drifted into "only Combat team players are ever paying attention," it's usually because non-Combat scenes are being narrated too briefly or resolved with too little stake. Give Recon, Research, Resource, and Support scenes at least one real decision point per session, not just a skill check and a one-line result.
Pacing a whole session. A reasonable default shape for a table session: roughly twenty minutes of setup and check-in, one moderate scene, one harder scene or fight, and a ten-to-fifteen-minute close where you name consequences and set up the next hook out loud. If a session runs long, cut from the middle scene, never from the close — players remember how a session ended far more than how it started.
Debrief. Spend the last five minutes, after the table has broken, jotting down what confused people. A rules explanation you had to give twice this session is a rules explanation you should pre-empt at the start of the next one.
Chapter 24 — Between Adventures
Not everything worth resolving happens at the table in real time. This chapter is your toolkit for the time between sessions — and for the Opening's own calendar (Chapter 3) ticking forward even when nobody's rolling dice.
Opening-calendar bookkeeping. Keep a simple running log: current day/hour inside the 2400-hour Opening, which tier the Expedition is pushing, and any standing faction or rumor beats you've planted (Chapter 12). Five minutes of upkeep between sessions keeps the world's clock honest without demanding a full simulation.
Reputation drift between sessions. Reputation shouldn't only move during dramatic table moments — let time and rumor move it too. A faction the party hasn't interacted with in several sessions can drift slightly toward Neutral; a faction whose interests the party's public actions have obviously served or harmed can drift without a direct interaction, as word travels through a haven's rumor mill.
Craft and research queues. Long crafting chains and deep research tasks are the textbook case for Between-Session resolution (Chapter 2): rather than spending table time on repeated checks with no dramatic content, resolve the whole chain between sessions with a short message or a quick pre-session check-in, and bring only the interesting outcome to the table.
Downtime jobs and Opening-side work. Between dungeon pushes, an Expedition (or individual characters) can take on haven-side work: guard contracts, escort jobs, minor faction favors. Treat these the same way as any other Quick Resolution activity — a check, a stated outcome, a small reputation or coin result — unless a player specifically wants to turn one into a full scene.
Rival Expeditions. Nothing sharpens stakes like knowing someone else is racing for the same Gate. Track one or two rival Expeditions in broad strokes — their public reputation, their last known depth, and one thing they want that overlaps with the party's own goals — and update their status between sessions the same way you update the party's.
Chapter 25 — Canon Consistency Protocol
Use this compact checklist for one-shots, playtests, campaign summaries, and any material another Director may inherit.
Always:
Respect the twelve-character Expedition cap, or explicitly label anything larger as a multi-Expedition coalition (Chapter 4).
Use the 2400-hour Opening and 1200-hour Horn clocks in Chapter 3.
Label provisional combat statistics, XP awards, and optional rules as playtest material.
Keep Silent Ones and Heartcell material (Chapter 8) out of any player-facing or newcomer-facing summary.
Use bestiary Gate Guardian names (Chapter 11), never retired cosmology-only names.
When presenting a full Expedition, give Reconnaissance, Research, Resource, and Support at least one meaningful logged outcome alongside Combat.
Never:
Invent foreign cosmology or borrow another product's named systems, brands, or terminology.
Silently rewrite the Player Rulebook or Director Manual to make an outcome fit.
Treat the Fifteenfold Pantheon, the Great Wheel, or any other mortal folk-religion model as objective cosmic truth.
Report Resonance as "spell slots" without noting the correction, even in passing.
When published texts conflict: use the player-facing rule for the current session, record the exact contradiction, and resolve it between sessions as errata or a clearly labeled table rule. Never conceal the change from the players.

PART V — BUILDING THE DESCENT
Chapter 27 — The Floor-Building Engine
A Decennium floor is not a collection of unrelated rooms. It is a pressure system with a recognizable identity, a route the Expedition can understand, a cost for learning that route, and at least one reason to return after the first visit. Build floors from function outward. Start with what the floor does to an Expedition, then decide what it looks like.
The Seven Required Elements
1. Threshold. A clear moment when the Expedition understands it has entered a new operational space. The threshold can be a stair, bridge, veil, flooded passage, or legal boundary inside a floor civilization.
2. Muster node. A place where the Expedition can reorganize teams, read the environment, and make an informed first choice. It need not be safe, but it must be legible.
3. Teaching pressure. An early hazard or encounter that demonstrates the floor’s dominant rule before that rule becomes lethal.
4. Branch. At least two meaningful routes. One should be faster and more dangerous; the other slower, more expensive, politically complicated, or uncertain.
5. Discovery. A reward that changes future decisions: a map, recipe, faction truth, shortcut, omen, or clue. Discovery is not merely treasure.
6. Escalation node. A place where the floor’s pressure becomes harder, stranger, or more personal. This is where a casual expedition realizes it must either commit or withdraw.
7. Exit and return logic. The party must know how it can leave, what may block withdrawal, and what will be different when it comes back.
The Floor Sentence
Before drawing a map, finish this sentence: “This floor forces the Expedition to _____ while _____ grows worse.” The first blank is the action; the second is the pressure. Examples include “navigate by sound while echoes attract predators,” “negotiate passage while an oath slowly binds the speakers,” or “cross exposed bridges while radiance strips protection from equipment.” If the sentence could describe any dungeon, the floor is not specific enough.
Node Maps Instead of Room Counts
Use five to nine important nodes for a standard floor. Corridors, empty chambers, and minor obstacles exist between them but do not need full keys unless they matter. A node deserves preparation when it contains a decision, cost, revelation, resource, or set piece. This keeps the map usable during play and leaves room for the dungeon to feel larger than the paper describing it.

The Return Value
Every floor should contain at least two reasons to return. One is visible during the first visit: a locked reliquary, incomplete translation, unharvested resource, or faction offer. The other becomes visible only after the party leaves: a rumor about what they missed, a buyer seeking a specific component, a rival claiming a route, or an item reacting to the floor from which it came. Return value makes the Decennium feel like a place rather than a disposable sequence of encounters.
Floor Canon Test
A new floor may be surreal, but it must still serve the tier’s dominant sensory idea, respect the ten-floor Gate structure, and avoid naming the Heartcell, Silent Ones, or Ul’nak in player-facing material. Deep clues may imply older architecture; they do not explain it.

Chapter 28 — Exploration Turns and Dungeon Pressure
The Player Rulebook divides expedition days into morning, afternoon, and evening periods. Inside a hazardous floor, the Director needs a smaller unit. Use the exploration turn: approximately ten minutes of careful movement, searching, treatment, harvesting, repair, or focused investigation. Six exploration turns equal one hour of the Opening. Do not force this procedure during free conversation or a dramatic scene; use it when time, noise, supply, or exposure matters.
The Exploration Sequence
1. Declare. The acting team states its objective and approach. “Search the western wall for a mechanical seam” is actionable; “I investigate everything” is not.
2. Assign time. Most focused actions take one turn. Complex treatment, harvesting, ritual work, or moving through severe terrain may take two or more.
3. Resolve. Make only the checks that uncertainty requires. A correct tool, specific description, or prior discovery may remove the need for a roll.
4. Advance pressure. Mark elapsed time and update any relevant tracks.
5. Reveal consequence. Give the result, including what the character learns about the risk. Failure should change the situation, not merely produce “nothing happens.”

Tracks are Director instruments, not universal player statistics. Tell players what their characters can perceive: the air is becoming harder to breathe, tracks show a patrol changing course, the lantern oil is below half. You may show the numerical track if your table prefers explicit resource play, but the fiction must remain primary.
Pressure Rolls
When a track rises and the next consequence is uncertain, roll a pressure die. Use d6 for early tiers, d8 for middle tiers, and d10 for Tiers 7–10. A result at or below the current track triggers a complication. The die does not decide whether the world reacts; it decides whether the reaction arrives now. A track at 6 triggers automatically and then falls by 2 after the consequence resolves, unless the situation clearly continues to worsen.
Searching and Information Fairness
Do not hide required progress behind one check. If the Expedition must find the Gate key to continue, place at least three independent leads to it. Checks determine speed, cost, completeness, or whether a complication occurs. They should not determine whether the campaign simply stops. Optional treasure, shortcuts, and deeper lore may remain missable.
Resting Inside the Decennium
A short or long rest is never free merely because the rules allow it. The party must have time and a plausible location. A long rest advances the Opening by eight hours, consumes supplies, and gives rivals and factions time to move. In an exposed location, the party may need to spend turns concealing the camp, setting watches, or bargaining for shelter. Denying every rest is punitive; granting every rest without consequence erases the expedition game.
Use Pressure to Make Choices, Not to Punish
Raise pressure when the Expedition takes actions that logically create it. Do not add Noise because the party succeeded too easily or increase Exposure because you want to force a fight. Pressure is credible only when players can understand what caused it and choose differently next time.

Step Three: Apply Role Pressure
Raw damage does not capture control, mobility, concealment, or action denial. Remove one step when the party has surprise, a strong chokepoint, a researched vulnerability, or allied support that can actually participate.
Step Four: Build Three Levers
Player lever. A feature the party can exploit: unstable pillars, exposed conduits, a frightened beast, a ritual phrase, or a route discovered by Reconnaissance.
Director lever. A believable adjustment available during play: morale, delayed reinforcements, shifting terrain, an objective changing, or a foe choosing capture over murder.
Exit lever. A path to retreat, negotiate, surrender something, or achieve a partial objective. Hard fights without exits become accidental executions.
Encounter Waves
Use waves when you need scale without exceeding the eight-actor rule. Prepare the total opposition, but begin with only the actors the party can perceive. Later waves enter because of a visible trigger: a bell, an opened passage, a Noise threshold, or a leader’s command. If the party prevents the trigger, it has earned a smaller encounter. Never add waves solely because the first group died quickly.
Bosses Without Hit-Point Walls
A major enemy should change the question at least once. Phase changes can alter terrain, reveal an objective, break an oath, expose a vulnerable component, or force the party to protect something. A phase is not simply “the boss uses stronger attacks below half HP.” The new phase should require a new decision.
Encounter Record
After a significant fight, record expected rounds, actual rounds, how many characters reached 0 HP, which abilities dominated, and whether players understood the objective. Three lines of data are more useful for future balance than a vague memory that the fight felt easy.

The best hazards announce enough of themselves that careful players can choose an approach, then remain dangerous because acting under pressure is difficult. Use traps to reveal the dungeon’s priorities: what it protects, what behavior it rewards, and what kind of expedition it was built to attract.

The Three Clues Rule
Before a serious trap, provide three types of clue whenever possible: environmental evidence, behavioral evidence, and structural evidence. Scorch marks are environmental. Chapter 31 — Camps, Havens, and Tier 6 Civilizations
A camp is an Expedition’s claim that a small piece of the Decennium can be made predictable. A haven is that claim made durable by many people. A civilization is a network of such claims with memory, law, exchange, and the power to refuse outsiders. Up through Tier 6, the depths can support all three. Past Tier 6, stable settlements become rare exceptions, temporary enclaves, or remnants whose continued survival should itself be a mystery.

What Makes a Haven Feel Real
A reason it exists. Water, a protected seam, trade, a broken Gate mechanism, pilgrimage, exile, salvage, or control of a route.
A price of survival. Tithe, silence, labor, restricted magic, ritual, hostage custom, or dependence on a dangerous resource.
A law outsiders will violate accidentally. The law should arise from local survival rather than arbitrary cruelty.
A thing the haven cannot make. This creates trade and gives Expeditions leverage.
A fear about the next Cycle. Havens remember that the Opening changes routes, markets, and who returns.
Tier 6 Civilizations
Tier 6 is the last depth where small stable civilizations may exist without being treated as a unique miracle. Their people are not waiting for surface adventurers to solve everything. They have laws, route knowledge, internal politics, and opinions about the Opening. Some may descend from trapped Expeditions; others may be native floor cultures, Pact-era custodial populations, or mixed communities that have survived many Cycles.
Do not use Tier 6 societies as exposition mouths for Director-secret cosmology. They may possess words such as Signatory, Bearer, Custodian, Thread, or First Architecture without understanding the whole. Their theology can be wrong in sophisticated ways. Knowledge becomes more compelling when a culture has built a life around a partial truth.
Haven Reaction Procedure
1. Identify the party’s visible burden. Weapons, wounded, forbidden relics, faction marks, and who speaks first all matter.
2. Apply reputation. Use the relevant faction or settlement standing, not a generic Charisma attitude.
3. Name the immediate concern. Every haven encounter begins with what the settlement needs or fears today.
4. Offer exchange. Information, access, treatment, supplies, or passage should have a price.
5. Record the precedent. A party that breaks quarantine, pays generously, or defends a gate changes how the haven behaves next time.
Civilization Boundary
Tiers 7–10 should not contain ordinary, comfortable cities by default. Any durable population there must be protected by a profound bargain, trapped in a repeating condition, transformed by the tier, or concealed behind a fact that becomes a campaign mystery.

Chapter 32 — Designing Gates and Unlock Conditions
A Gate is the final examination of a tier. It asks whether the Expedition understood the place it crossed, paid attention to its people and hazards, and is willing to accept the cost of going deeper. The Guardian is part of the seal, but defeating the Guardian by damage alone should not always be sufficient.
The Six Gate Components
1. Approach. A final route that gathers the tier’s themes and gives the party time to prepare.
2. Witness. Someone or something that observes entry: a faction, mechanism, choir, reflection, or remembered oath.
3. Requirement. Knowledge, objects, actions, or relationships earned on the tier.
4. Cost. Time, resources, reputation, a promise, exposure, or a changed item.
5. Guardian condition. What must be done during or around the confrontation beyond reducing HP.
6. Aftermark. A title, scar, key, resonance change, faction reaction, or visible alteration to the Gate.

No Single-Roll Gates
A Gate may include checks, but no single check should decide whether an entire campaign continues. Provide multiple ways to satisfy the requirement, allow failures to increase cost or change the confrontation, and make research reduce uncertainty. A party that reaches the Gate without the intended key should be able to retreat, bargain, improvise at a severe price, or discover a secondary path.
Gate Knowledge Distribution
Place one clue in the environment, one in an NPC or rival relationship, one in a recoverable record, and one in the behavior of the tier’s hazards. The Expedition should be able to assemble the truth from different team contributions. This is where the twelve-seat structure earns its place: Combat may hold the approach, Reconnaissance finds the seam, Research reads the sequence, Resource prepares the material, and Support keeps the ritual intact.
Opening a Gate Changes the Tier
The Gate should not simply disappear. Patrol patterns shift. A haven celebrates or panics. A trade route opens. A rival gains access because the party did the hard work. The Guardian’s death or release may damage a ward the tier depended on. Record at least two changes and one new opportunity after every Gate victory.
Chapter 33 — Guardian Encounters as Seal Set Pieces
Gate Guardians are rules of the Heartcell made physical, though no player-facing description should say so. Their encounters combine arena, ritual, objective, and combat. The full creature statistics live in the Codex of the Heartcell; this chapter provides the structure that makes those statistics matter.
The Five-Phase Seal Structure
1. Recognition. The Guardian identifies, measures, or names the Expedition through the tier’s logic. Players should understand what the confrontation is about before initiative.
2. First law. The Guardian demonstrates its central rule in a survivable form.
3. Contradiction. The party discovers how the arena, requirement, or Guardian can be turned against itself.
4. Seal crisis. At least one non-damage objective becomes urgent: hold a note, forge a key, keep an oath, protect a witness, align a reflection, or prevent a reset.
5. Aftermark. Victory changes the Gate and marks the Expedition. The aftermath is played, not summarized.
Guardian Action Economy
A Guardian facing four to six detailed actors needs meaningful presence between its turns. Use reactions, arena pulses, triggered movement, or a lair-like event, but avoid stacking so many effects that players cannot identify cause and response. One Guardian action, one arena event, and one reaction family is usually enough. Additional complexity should come from objectives, not from a page of unrelated attacks.
Seal Failure States
Failure
Result
Combat defeat
Death, capture, expulsion, bargain, or retreat depending on the Guardian’s nature and the party’s choices
Objective failure
The Guardian heals, the arena resets, the cost increases, or the Gate remains closed even if the body falls
Requirement violation
A faction withdraws recognition, a mark becomes a curse, or the next tier opens under worse conditions
Time failure
The confrontation continues, but the Opening, rival, or seal clock reaches a consequence

Mercy, Bargain, and Replacement
Not every Guardian must die. A seal may be satisfied through surrender, recognition, release, or replacement. If a party proposes an answer that fits the tier’s logic better than the prepared solution, allow it at a meaningful cost. The dungeon is engineered, not arbitrary. Its rules can be understood and exploited.
Guardian Canon
Use only the canonical roster: Vaskor, the Bloom That Hungers, Ashthirst, Gor Vekk, Choralith, Lumen, Xarog, Eirfax, Threxus, and the Marquis of Silence. Retired cosmology names may survive as mortal legends or mistaken titles, but they are not the playable Guardian roster.

Chapter 34 — The Old God Interstice
Between the Pactbound Arena and the Duskshard Pools lies an interruption in the ten-tier rhythm: the Old God Interstice. It is not Tier 7.5, not an eleventh Gate, and not a conventional floor. It is a place where the descent encounters a being or remnant whose existence does not fit the public architecture of the Crucible.
What the Interstice Must Accomplish
Break confidence. The Expedition should realize that its existing maps, Gate assumptions, and public theology are incomplete.
Change scale. The party has been surviving a dungeon. The Interstice suggests that the dungeon is surviving something else.
Preserve mystery. It may reveal an Old God, but it should not explain the Silent Ones, Heartcell, or Matrix in a clean lecture.
Demand a cost. Passage requires memory, identity, a promise, a relinquished relic, a changed relationship, or another consequence that survives the scene.
Create a before and after. Characters who cross should not discuss the lower tiers with the same certainty they had above.

Information Limits
The Interstice can confirm that something predates the public Crucible, that the Pact’s sovereigns did not originate every mechanism, and that names have been deliberately buried. It should not hand players a complete cosmology. Use images that rhyme with the Matrix: choices collapsing, histories merging, names becoming interchangeable, sound losing distinction. Let players infer that stillness is dangerous before they know why.
Crossing Consequences
Each character should leave with one personal consequence. Examples: a memory that no longer belongs only to them, a name the character cannot speak, a scar that resonates near deep architecture, a lost certainty about a deity, or a promise witnessed by something outside the Pact. These consequences are not random curses; they are campaign tools that make Tier 8 onward feel different.
DIRECTOR-SECRET: Director-Secret Interpretation
The Interstice is where the Heartcell’s prison function can become perceptible without becoming fully explained. The Old God may be captive, wardened, feeding, dreaming, or partially integrated into the mechanism. Choose one truth for the campaign and keep the others as contradictory evidence.

Chapter 35 — Relics, Rewards, and the Bait Economy
Loot is the Heartcell’s recruitment system, but rewards must still feel desirable. The Director’s task is not to punish players for taking treasure. It is to make treasure consequential enough that carrying it creates stories on the surface and in the depths.
The Four-Part Relic
1. Promise. What the item obviously allows the character to do.
2. Behavior. What the item encourages the wielder to repeat.
3. Audience. Who notices, recognizes, desires, fears, or claims it.
4. Return. What changes if it is brought back to the floor, Gate, faction, or depth from which it came.

Reward Mix
For every three significant rewards, aim for one practical item, one information reward, and one relationship or access reward. A party that receives only weapons learns that only Combat matters. Maps, research privileges, recipes, camp rights, titles, transport agreements, and the loyalty of a specialist should have equal narrative weight.
Selling Dungeon-Touched Goods
A sale creates at least three records: who bought it, who learned it was sold, and what the buyer intends to do next. High-value relics rarely vanish into a merchant’s inventory. They become research projects, symbols, weapons, scandals, or theft targets. When players fence an item secretly, determine which part of the secrecy holds and which part leaks.
Destroying Relics
Destroying an important relic should be possible, but never trivial. Require a method, location, or sacrifice that reflects the item. Destruction can reduce the Heartcell’s bait network, release stored resonance, anger a faction, or open an unexpected path. Do not automatically treat destruction as morally superior; it is another consequential choice.

Rivals make the Opening move when the player characters are not looking. They are most effective when they are competent, understandable, and capable of cooperation as well as betrayal. A rival that exists only to steal kills or mock the party will become tedious. A rival with overlapping goals creates decisions.

PART VI — CAMPAIGNS ACROSS THE CYCLE
Chapter 37 — Campaign Formats
Decennium Descent can support a single desperate push, a full 2400-hour Opening, or a legacy campaign spanning several Cycles. Choose the format before Session Zero because it determines how hard the clocks press, how much surface play matters, and whether mysteries are expected to resolve.

The Campaign Promise
Tell players what the campaign is promising. “We are playing a hard expedition where retreat is success” creates different expectations from “we are following one group toward Floor 100.” The promise can change, but change it openly. Do not lure players into a political haven campaign and then treat every social investment as wasted because only the next Gate matters.
Depth Is Not the Only Progress
Track four campaign advances: depth, knowledge, infrastructure, and influence. An Expedition may spend sessions building a safe route, earning a Tier 4 haven’s trust, or identifying a Gate condition without descending a floor. These are real advances because they change future costs. A campaign that measures progress only by floor number will undervalue most of its own systems.
Arc Rhythm
A reliable rhythm is push, return, consequence, preparation, and push again. Deep campaigns may stretch the return over several sessions or use a haven instead of the surface. Every arc should change at least one of the four advances and move at least one rival or faction clock.
Chapter 38 — The 2400-Hour Opening Event Engine
The Opening is one hundred days, measured as 2400 hours. The Horn sounds at hour 1200. Treat the timeline as a public campaign object. Players should know the current hour and what major deadlines have been announced. Hidden clocks may exist, but the fundamental Opening clock is never a surprise.
Opening Pulses
At the start of every 24-hour block, resolve an Opening Pulse. Choose or roll one surface event, one depth event, and one faction movement. Only events relevant to the party need immediate scenes; the rest become rumors or changes waiting for discovery.

Event Selection
Choose events that touch existing decisions. If the party sold a radiant relic, the event is a forge-guild demonstration or theft, not an unrelated earthquake. If the party ignored a missing Reconnaissance team, the event is a survivor appearing with half a map. The engine is not a random distraction generator; it pays off what the campaign has already planted.
The Horn
At hour 1200, stop and stage the Horn as a scene even if the party is in combat, negotiation, or sleep. It may arrive as vibration, distant brass, pressure in the teeth, a synchronized reaction from wards, or a cultural ritual in a haven. Every major NPC should reveal what the Horn means to them. Afterward, ask the Expedition directly: are you now planning to extract, hold a haven, or continue downward? The answer is not binding, but it makes the risk conscious.
The Final Twelve Hours
Do not fill the final twelve hours with arbitrary extra encounters. Use obstacles already earned: an injured ally, a rival’s plea, a route damaged earlier, a relic that will not cross a threshold cleanly, or a faction demanding payment. Telegraph travel time exactly enough that players can choose whom and what to save.
Chapter 39 — The Revelation Ladder
The Director-secret cosmology should be discoverable, but never cheap. Use the four-rung ladder established in Chapter 8 and expand each rung across several pieces of evidence. A revelation is earned when players connect information through action, not when an NPC decides it is time for a lore speech.
Rung One: Impossible Age
Evidence suggests parts of the architecture predate the public creation story. Use mismatched dating, scripts layered over older geometry, tools that cannot work the material they supposedly built, or a floor culture whose oldest songs already describe the Crucible as ancient. The party still has many reasonable explanations: divine time, lost precursors, or symbolic history.
Rung Two: Missing Names
Records use titles such as Custodian, Bearer, Signatory, or Keeper without naming Calan’thir or Asmodeus. A seal may recognize an office rather than a deity. A public hymn may have been altered. The discovery should create a question about governance before it creates a conclusion about origin.
Rung Three: Governance, Not Origin
The party finds a statement, mechanism, or witness demonstrating that the Divine Pact regulates something older. This is the campaign’s first load-bearing contradiction. The sovereigns remain genuine builders of the public Crucible, but they did not create every underlying system.
Rung Four: Contact With the Operating Cycle
The Expedition encounters the Heartcell’s function directly: resonance routing, reward replenishment, Gate victory as fuel, or a mechanism reacting to conflict and difference. At this rung the party can know the prison exists, though it may still misunderstand the prisoner, the wardens, or the Matrix.

Failed Interpretations Are Play
Let players form wrong theories that fit the evidence. Do not immediately correct them. Introduce future evidence honestly and allow theories to evolve. A false theory becomes harmful only when the Director changes established facts merely to surprise the table.
The World Does Not Learn Automatically
Even when the player characters reach rung four, Aethel does not immediately accept their discovery. Evidence may be dangerous, politically suppressed, impossible to reproduce outside the Opening, or interpreted through the Fifteenfold and Great Wheel folk models.

Chapter 40 — Multi-Cycle Play and Legacy
A Cycle is ten years. That interval is long enough for children to become adventurers, rivals to become patrons, settlements to rise or fail, and a relic’s surface history to matter. Multi-Cycle play turns individual expeditions into institutions and consequences into inheritance.

Cycle Start Procedure
1. Name the previous Cycle. Use the event or person history remembers, not necessarily what the players think mattered most.
2. Resolve five legacies. Choose one person, institution, route, relic, and truth from the prior campaign and state how each changed over ten years.
3. Choose three reversals. A safe haven failed, a hated faction became necessary, a lost rival returned, or a public hero’s story changed.
4. Advance the dungeon. Move one route, alter one floor pressure, and change one Gate consequence. Preserve enough continuity that old maps still matter imperfectly.
5. Create inheritance hooks. New characters begin with debts, names, objects, mentors, or enemies connected to earlier play.
Playing Trapped Characters
Characters sealed inside may become protagonists of an inter-Cycle campaign, legendary NPCs, or altered survivors discovered ten years later. Decide whether time passes normally for them. Different depths may support different answers, but establish the local rule before it becomes convenient. Survival should require communities, bargains, transformation, or access to resources; ten years cannot be hand-waved as an unusually long rest.
Legacy Characters
A descendant or student should inherit context, not a duplicate build. Give one connection benefit: a known contact, a partial map, a recognized name, a family relic, or starting reputation. Then give one burden: debt, expectation, enemy, missing truth, or disputed claim. Legacy works when inheritance creates decisions.
Chapter 41 — Death, Replacement, and Expedition Continuity
Death in Decennium Descent is mechanically clear in the Player Rulebook and socially complicated in an Expedition. The Director must protect both consequence and continuity. A dead character should matter, but one player should not be excluded from the campaign for several sessions while the party debates logistics.
At the Moment of Death
1. Resolve the rules plainly. Confirm the damage, death-save state, and features that could intervene. Do not obscure the result for drama.
2. Pause the fiction. Give the table a moment before looting, moving, or arguing.
3. Ask for immediate intent. Recover the body, continue the objective, retreat, perform a rite, or leave a mark.
4. Record witnesses and possessions. These become later reputation, inheritance, and bait consequences.

The Body as a Campaign Object
Recovery may matter because of religion, family, evidence, inheritance, resurrection methods, or what dungeon-touched equipment remains attached. Do not force every group into body-recovery play. Ask at Session Zero how the table treats remains, funerals, and the possibility of undeath. When recovery is chosen, make the objective meaningful but not grotesquely punitive.
Near-Death Without Cheapening Death
Capture, scarring, lost equipment, oath-bound release, and separation can replace death when they arise credibly from enemy motives. They should not be used secretly to guarantee survival. Players need to believe the rules and choices can kill characters, even when not every defeat does.
Chapter 42 — Surface Politics, War, and the Divine Pact
The surface exists to receive what the dungeon sends back: wealth, survivors, stories, weapons, panic, and arguments about what the Opening means. Politics should not be a pause from the descent. It is the second half of the bait cycle.
Political Consequence Procedure
1. Identify the changed resource. A route, relic, map, survivor, proof, or public story.
2. Name three interested groups. One wants control, one wants suppression, and one wants access.
3. Choose the public question. Who owns it? Is it safe? Who deserves credit? What law applies?
4. Choose the hidden question. Who benefits if conflict continues? Who fears the truth reducing conflict or exposing the Pact?
5. Put a face on each side. Politics becomes playable through people with wants and leverage.
War as Dissonance
The Divine Pact’s deeper function does not mean every war is secretly ordered by Heaven or Hell. Most conflict is mortal, sincere, and destructive. The setting’s horror is that the machinery benefits from difference and struggle regardless of who began it. Keep responsibility with the characters and institutions that choose violence. Cosmic function is not moral absolution.
Pact Intervention
Direct divine or infernal intervention should be rare, formal, and constrained. Agents prefer doctrine, contracts, omens, legal rulings, chosen champions, and faction pressure. When a being tied to the Pact acts openly, show the cost of acting without breaking the agreement. This preserves the setting’s bureaucratic liturgy and prevents cosmology from becoming arbitrary omnipotence.
Folk Religion
The Fifteenfold Pantheon and Great Wheel may shape law, ritual, class identity, and culture. Treat them as mortal religious frameworks containing real experiences and incomplete interpretations. NPCs can be devout without being secretly foolish. Their rites may work because divine powers answer, because the Weave responds, because institutions preserve effective practices, or because several explanations overlap.
Chapter 43 — Downtime, Craft, and Research Chains
The Player Rulebook supplies the foundations of harvesting and crafting. The Director’s task is to turn those procedures into campaign choices. A chain should begin with a question or desired capability, require access and components, consume Opening time or inter-adventure time, and produce both an answer and a consequence.
Research Chain
1. Question. State what the researchers want to know in one sentence.
2. Sources. Require two or three source types: physical evidence, records, witnesses, experiments, or comparative samples.
3. Obstacle. Missing language, dangerous handling, political restriction, contamination, contradiction, or time.
4. Breakthrough. The answer changes a future decision, reduces a Gate cost, reveals a vulnerability, or creates a new question.
5. Exposure. Someone learns the research exists, an object reacts, or the process changes the researchers.
Craft Chain
1. Function. What problem the item solves.
2. Formula. Who knows it or where it can be learned.
3. Components. One common base, one tier-specific component, and one limiting component or service.
4. Facility. Tools, haven access, forge, laboratory, or ritual space.
5. Test. A check or choice that determines quality, complication, time, or side effect—not whether all materials simply vanish for nothing.
Opening-Time Crafting
Crafting inside the 2400-hour Opening trades immediate depth for future efficiency. Make that trade visible. A team spending six hours building spore filters should know the rival clock advances, but also know exactly what protection the filters provide. Do not reduce every project to one generic roll.
Failed Research
Failure should produce a partial answer, false confidence with visible warning signs, damaged evidence, faction attention, or a requirement for a new source. Pure “no result” wastes table time and discourages non-Combat roles.
Chapter 44 — Social Conflict and Reputation Scenes
A social scene becomes a conflict when someone wants an outcome the other side has reason to resist. Roleplay determines approach and information; checks resolve uncertainty. Reputation determines the starting field and what outcomes are plausible. A Honored Expedition may be heard without an appointment. A Hated one may need leverage merely to enter the room.

Three Results, Not Two
Prepare a full success, costly success, and refusal or reversal. Costly success is not a disguised failure; the party gets the requested access or agreement but owes a favor, accepts oversight, loses secrecy, pays more, or grants credit. Refusal should open another path: new leverage, a different faction, illegal entry, or delayed opportunity.
Using Skills
Persuasion changes willingness, Deception changes believed information, Intimidation changes perceived risk, and Insight changes what the character understands. No single skill controls another person’s mind. A perfect argument cannot make an NPC betray the one value the scene established as non-negotiable, though it may reveal what would change that limit.
Reputation Movement
Move reputation after actions that are public, significant, and attributable. Private kindness may change one NPC without changing a faction. A witnessed Gate victory, broken treaty, rescue, theft, or public accusation can shift a tier. Record why the movement occurred; later NPCs should react to the story, not the number alone.
Chapter 45 — Directing Advanced and Deep-Tier Expeditions
High-level characters possess broad movement, strong defenses, rapid information tools, and combinations that can bypass ordinary obstacles. Do not respond by making every door immune, every enemy omniscient, or every clue magically unreadable. Deep play challenges priorities, scale, and consequences rather than merely increasing numbers.
Design for Capabilities
Teleportation and flight. Make destination knowledge, moving geometry, cargo, separated teams, and consequences of bypass matter. Let movement abilities remain powerful.
Divination. Answer honestly within the spell or feature’s scope. The challenge can be interpretation, competing futures, protected offices, or what acting on the answer changes.
Resurrection and death prevention. Use access, time, body recovery, faction law, and what returns with the character as story questions. Do not arbitrarily switch abilities off.
Mass damage. Use objectives, dispersed threats, moral and structural consequences, and enemies who value survival. Do not inflate every minor creature’s HP.
Social dominance. Powerful reputations attract formal obligations, rivals, scrutiny, and requests proportional to the Expedition’s status.
Deep-Tier Failure
At Tiers 7–10, failure can alter identity, oaths, maps, memories, and relationships. Keep consequences specific and playable. “The Spiral changes the route so your map now leads rivals correctly but you incorrectly” creates a campaign problem. “Reality is weird and nothing can be trusted” removes agency.
Power Deserves Witnesses
When players use legendary abilities, let the world notice. Floor cultures name them, rivals adapt, factions seek them, and relics respond. Recognition is not punishment; it is the setting acknowledging earned power.
PART VII — THE CYCLE OF THE ASHEN BELL
This part is a complete ready-to-run Tier 1 campaign. It begins at Opening hour 0, carries an Expedition through Floors 1–10 of the Yieldheart Caverns, and ends at Vaskor’s Seal. It is written for a new table but can be scaled for experienced characters by replacing combat statistics while preserving objectives and consequences. Full monster statistics should be drawn from the Codex of the Heartcell or the nearest appropriate bestiary entries.
Chapter 46 — Campaign Overview
The Public Situation
At Crystalfront, a weathered bronze bell called the Ashen Bell hangs over the eastern muster court. It was recovered three Cycles ago from the Yieldheart Caverns and has never rung except during an Opening. This Cycle, it rings thirteen times instead of ten. On the final toll, a hairline crack appears across its face and a gray crystal splinter falls into the crowd.
Three interests immediately form around the event. The Crystalfront Authority wants order and legal control of the fallen splinter. The Surveyors’ Collegium believes the extra toll marks a route change in the first tier. The Reliquary Brokers want the splinter quietly purchased before anyone establishes its value. The player Expedition is drawn in because one of its members catches the splinter, is struck by a memory-like echo, or is named by an injured surveyor who expected the bell to crack.
Campaign Objectives
Immediate. Enter the Yieldheart Caverns, confirm whether the first routes changed, and recover the brass seal of Surveyor Maelin Voss from the Abandoned Stoneworks on Floor 3.
Tier. Learn why crystal growth is spreading through old ward-lines, establish a reliable route through Floors 1–9, and open Vaskor’s Seal at Floor 10.
Political. Decide who controls the Ashen Bell splinter, the recovered maps, and credit for reopening the route.
Secret. Discover evidence that some underlying stonework predates the public inscriptions without learning the full Director-secret cosmology.
The Truth Behind the Ashen Bell
DIRECTOR-SECRET: Campaign Truth
The Ashen Bell was cast by Pact-era engineers around a fragment of older resonance material. It reacts when the Tier 1 seal redistributes pressure. The thirteenth toll indicates that an old maintenance route has reconnected to the active floor network. Maelin Voss discovered the route during the previous Cycle and hid her brass survey seal rather than let a patron claim it. The seal can unlock a safer approach to Vaskor, but it also identifies its bearer to older mechanisms as a temporary Surveyor.

Chapter 47 — Opening Day at Crystalfront
Scene One: The Muster Court
Read or paraphrase: “The Shardspire catches the dawn before the sun clears the eastern roofs. Every crystal edge answers with a pale line of fire. Twelve muster lanes face the sealed arch, crowded with banners, carts, priests, hired blades, scholars, and families pretending not to count the people they may never see again. Above the eastern court hangs the Ashen Bell. It has not moved in ten years.”
Ask each player what their character carried into the court that proves they intend to return. This can be a letter, empty reliquary, debt marker, family token, map case, or promise. Record the answers. They become useful when the Horn sounds and during Vaskor’s Recognition phase.
The Thirteenth Toll
The bell rings once for each tier. The first ten tolls are expected. The eleventh and twelfth create confusion. The thirteenth cracks the bell. Choose the character who receives the splinter based on position or ask for a Dexterity save only if several characters plausibly reach it. The splinter is warm, then suddenly cold. The bearer hears a hammer striking stone from below and sees, for one breath, a brass disk beneath a collapsed forge bench.

Chapter 51 — The Warded Gate of Floor 3
The Warded Gate is not a tier Gate and has no Gate Guardian. It ends the automaton safety net and teaches the table how the Decennium uses earned knowledge. Beyond it, Floors 4–10 no longer benefit from routine Pact sentinel intervention.
The Four Principles
Four rotating glyph rings represent Burden, Witness, Measure, and Return. The principles were demonstrated on Floors 1–3: name what you carry, allow passage to be witnessed, measure rather than merely take, and preserve a route back. The party can learn the correct relationships from the threshold phrase, stamped stones, cancellation sequence, Voss seal, and foreman ledger.
Solving the Gate
1. Burden. Place or name one item the Expedition accepts responsibility for carrying.
2. Witness. A person outside the active Combat team must observe and record the opening.
3. Measure. Use the Voss seal, a carefully built substitute, or a costly calibration using resonant salts.
4. Return. Mark the route behind and leave a token intended for those who come after.
A party missing one principle may substitute time and cost. For example, without the Voss seal, Research can reconstruct the measure over four hours with salts and Stoneworks tools. Without an outside witness, an automaton can be repaired or a rival invited under negotiated terms. The Gate never depends on one die roll.

PART VIII — DIRECTOR’S TABLECRAFT
Chapter 54 — Expectations, Safety, and Campaign Boundaries
A dangerous setting works only when the players trust the table. Session Zero establishes what characters may suffer, what players do not want described, how conflict within the Expedition works, and how anyone can pause play. These procedures are not opposed to horror or consequence; they make both possible without relying on surprise discomfort.
Required Session-Zero Agreements
Campaign promise. Expected length, likely depth, lethality, and balance between dungeon, surface, and political play.
Character conflict. Whether theft, coercion, secret betrayal, alignment incompatibility, and player-versus-player combat are allowed and under what consent.
Content boundaries. Subjects excluded entirely, subjects allowed only off-screen, and subjects that require a check-in.
Death and body treatment. Lethality, resurrection, undeath, funerals, and how quickly replacement characters enter.
Secrets. Whether players may keep character secrets from one another and whether the Director may pass private information.
Pause signal. A word, gesture, or card that stops or rewinds a scene without requiring public justification.
Alignment Compatibility
The Player Rulebook treats Good and Evil characters as unable to form a stable Expedition together. Enforce that rule during character creation rather than discovering the conflict after play begins. Neutral characters may bridge moral differences, but the table still needs agreement on methods. Alignment does not excuse one player from respecting another player’s boundaries.
Horror Through Choice
Describe enough for players to understand risk, then let them choose how closely to engage. A sealed corpse may be investigated through medical detail, religious rite, distant tools, or not at all. Horror becomes stronger when the group chooses to look rather than being trapped in unwanted description.
Check-Ins After Major Scenes
After character death, betrayal, severe body transformation, or a revelation that changes a character’s faith or identity, take a brief out-of-character check. Ask whether everyone is comfortable continuing with the consequence as presented. This does not invite players to erase every setback; it confirms that the fiction remains playable.
Chapter 55 — Rules Disputes and Consistent Rulings
During play, speed and consistency matter more than perfect interpretation. Use the Player Rulebook first, then this manual’s Director procedures, then a temporary ruling. Record temporary rulings that may recur. Review them between sessions rather than turning the table into a design meeting.
The Sixty-Second Ruling
1. State the question. Reduce the dispute to one sentence.
2. Find the nearest rule. Use the closest existing action, check, save, condition, or resource procedure.
3. Name the stakes. Tell the player what success and failure mean before the roll.
4. Rule for this scene. Mark it as temporary if uncertain.
5. Review later. Change future rulings openly; do not retroactively erase a fair result unless a serious error harmed the table.
When Rules and Fiction Pull Apart
Keep the mechanical effect unless the fiction makes it impossible, and explain the fiction honestly. If a feature says it works on a visible target and the target is not visible, it does not work. If the feature works and the Director merely dislikes how easily it solves a prepared obstacle, it works. Build the next obstacle with the capability in mind.
Player Rulebook Authority
When this manual summarizes a player-facing rule, the Player Rulebook remains authoritative. Do not silently use a Director summary to reduce a class feature, change a spell, or alter character creation. When a genuine conflict appears, identify it, rule temporarily, and flag it for manuscript correction.

Campaigns stall because the objective becomes unclear, players lose connection to consequences, logistics consume all table time, or the group no longer wants the campaign originally promised. Do not solve every stall by adding a mysterious attack. Diagnose the missing motion.

The Reckoning Session
When several arcs have become tangled, run a reckoning session centered on the surface or a haven. Bring back three NPCs, resolve two outstanding consequences, reveal one rival success, and ask the Expedition to choose one objective for the next push. This is not filler. It converts accumulated play into a new direction.
Changing the Campaign Promise
If the table no longer wants a Floor 100 descent, let the campaign become a Gate arc, a haven defense, a multi-Cycle institution story, or a deliberate extraction. Finishing the chosen story is better than forcing the original plan until the group stops playing.
Chapter 57 — Closing the Opening
An Opening ends in extraction, entrapment, death, victory, transformation, or a deliberate decision to remain. The final session should resolve the clock and immediate survival, then show how the world receives what the Expedition became.
Extraction Ending
Resolve the route, who makes it, what is abandoned, and what crosses the surface threshold. Then stage reception: family, patrons, law, healers, rivals, buyers, and the public story. Ask each player what their character says first when someone asks, “Was it worth it?”
Entrapment Ending
At hour 2400, the seal is not an instant death effect. State what closes, what still functions, and what immediate shelter or danger exists. Decide whether the next campaign follows the trapped Expedition, jumps ten years, or ends on the promise that someone will return. Entrapment should feel like a transformed campaign, not a punishment for missing a hidden timer.
Gate or Marquis Victory
Victory opens more than a door. Resolve the tier’s political and metaphysical consequences, who claims credit, what the Guardian’s absence changes, and which deeper question replaces the solved one. Floor 100 should not end with a treasure chest and silence. The Marquis encounter must force an answer about difference, stillness, and what the characters are willing to preserve.
Epilogue Questions

Which relic remains in circulation?
Who was left below, by choice or necessity?
What faction gained power from the result?

What name will history give this Cycle?
Final Director Principle
The dungeon is not meaningful because it is unbeatable. It is meaningful because every victory changes who returns, what the world desires, and which differences the characters choose to preserve.

PART IX — OPERATING EVERY TIER
The Tier Gazetteer names the dominant image, key locations, and canonical Guardian of each division. This part turns those summaries into operational guidance. It does not replace the Chronicle of the Decennial Opening or the Codex of the Heartcell. It tells a Director what kinds of decisions, pressures, communities, and revelations make each tier play differently.
Chapter 58 — Tier 1 Operations: Yieldheart Caverns
Director Purpose
Yieldheart teaches the language of the game: preparation creates leverage, treasure attracts attention, maps are valuable, the Expedition is larger than the Combat team, and the safety net has a visible edge. The tier should feel dangerous to the unprepared without feeling malicious toward new players. Its architecture is old, but its public Pact inscriptions remain common enough that the Crucible myth feels stable.
Core Pressures
Weight. Burden, ownership, unstable stone, pressure plates, and equipment decisions should recur in different forms.
Echo. Sound reveals routes and attracts threats. Let players learn to use echoes rather than treating noise only as punishment.
Claim. Salvage, inherited maps, abandoned camps, and automaton procedures establish that taking something creates responsibility.
Diminishing supervision. The first three floors contain visible Pact sentinels; beyond the Warded Gate their absence should be felt.

Encounters
Use creatures with clear motivations: crystal feeders defend mineral growth, burrowers react to vibration, automata enforce specific procedures, and scavengers retreat when denied food. The first tier should demonstrate that not every foe fights to the death. Include terrain that lets players redirect weight, break crystal, create echoes, or retreat into a mapped passage.
Revelation Ceiling
Tier 1 may reveal older seams beneath public inscriptions, unnamed offices, and records whose counting systems predate accepted history. It should not establish that Calan’thir and Asmodeus found an existing machine. A player who leaves Tier 1 should be able to say, “The construction story is incomplete,” not “We know who really built it.”
Gate Preparation
Vaskor should test what the Expedition carried, claimed, abandoned, and returned. Seed those decisions on every floor. By Floor 9, the party should possess at least one physical burden, one social obligation, one route investment, and one disputed claim that can appear during the Seal encounter.
Sample Floor Seed: The Quarry of Borrowed Names
Every tool taken from the quarry stamps its former owner’s name across the user’s skin in mineral dust. The name grants skill with the tool but also transfers one unfinished obligation. A haven needs the tools returned; a rival wants the expertise; and one name belongs to a person who officially never entered the Decennium.
Chapter 59 — Tier 2 Operations: Bloomdeep Marsh
Director Purpose
Bloomdeep removes the last expectation that stone corridors define the dungeon. It is an ecosystem with weather, food chains, contamination, and refuges hidden by living growth. The tier tests whether the Expedition can survive without treating every plant, fungus, and creature as an enemy or resource pile.
Core Pressures
Moisture and contamination. Supplies rot, wounds become vulnerable, and clean water requires judgment.
Visibility. Mist, reeds, spores, and reflected glow make distance uncertain.
Living routes. Paths grow closed, migrate, or open according to heat, sound, and harvesting.
Mutual dependence. Floor cultures and creatures may control remedies the party cannot replace from the surface.
Bloomdeep Travel
Maps should record conditions as well as geometry: water height, spore color, wind direction, bloom cycle, and what was disturbed. A route can remain geographically correct while becoming operationally useless. Reconnaissance earns its value by predicting when a path will be passable, not merely drawing it.
Hazard Design
Spore hazards need visible signs and varied responses. Burning may clear one cloud but awaken root systems or destroy medicine. Water may suppress spores but spread them downstream. Masks help until filters saturate. Give Resource and Research teams opportunities to identify, cultivate, neutralize, or deliberately redirect growth.
Communities
Use hidden refuges, amphibian enclaves, Mycelian networks, trapped surface descendants, or mixed salvage communities. Their laws should focus on quarantine, water rights, and what may be burned. Outsiders who ignore a contamination rule threaten everyone, giving social conflict immediate survival stakes.
Revelation Ceiling
Bloomdeep can show that the tier’s ecology is managed. Some species appear designed to convert death and conflict into new growth. Ancient irrigation routes may lead toward mechanisms no current culture understands. Do not name the Heartcell; let the party wonder whether the ecosystem is natural, divine, or engineered.
Gate Preparation
The Bloom That Hungers should be foreshadowed as both predator and regulator. Heartroot Blossoms need not be simple keys. Each may embody a different relationship to the marsh: cultivated, stolen, earned from a refuge, harvested from a dangerous corpse, or grown by sacrificing another resource. The party’s acquisition methods should change the Guardian confrontation.
Sample Floor Seed: The Lantern Mire
Floating lights mark safe ground, but each light contains a memory absorbed from a drowned traveler. Following a useful memory strengthens the light and erases part of the dead person’s identity. A family on the surface wants the memory preserved; a refuge needs the lights to keep a route open.
Chapter 60 — Tier 3 Operations: Anvil of Brogi
Director Purpose
The Anvil turns survival into industrial timing. Heat is constant, but the real challenge is managing processes: cooling cycles, pressure releases, moving machinery, material states, and work that cannot be paused safely. This tier should reward teams that understand systems rather than simply resist fire damage.
Core Pressures
Heat budget. Protection degrades, water becomes strategic, and exertion matters.
Timing. Crossings, vents, hammers, lifts, and pours follow intervals players can learn.
Equipment stress. Metal softens, leather chars, crystal fractures, and repairs become urgent.
Production claims. Forges, formulas, and finished work are controlled by guilds, spirits, or old contracts.
Running Industrial Hazards
State the cycle before asking for action. “The hammer falls every third round” creates play; “a hammer randomly crushes someone” creates caution without strategy. Let players stop, accelerate, redirect, or exploit machinery. A successful intervention should change the room permanently and may create salvage, a shortcut, or a new danger.
Forge Societies
Dwarven descendants, salamander-like cultures, infernal contractors, free constructs, and trapped craft families can all plausibly exist here. Their politics revolve around fuel, formulas, labor, ownership, and whether the Anvil is sacred, stolen, or merely useful. A community may be lawful without being aligned with Hell and may invoke Brogi as a folk or historical figure rather than objective cosmology.
Revelation Ceiling
Tier 3 may reveal that older machinery accepts offices and process states rather than prayers. Public dedications can be bolted onto systems that continue operating when the names are removed. This supports the idea that the Pact claimed and repurposed existing infrastructure without proving its full origin.
Gate Preparation
Ashthirst the Hollow should test whether the Expedition understands creation, consumption, and what remains after useful heat is spent. The key forged in Brogi’s Quenching Pool should bear the consequence of how its materials were obtained. A key made from stolen tools, sacrificed heirlooms, Guardian ore, or rival salvage should not open the same aftermath.
Sample Floor Seed: The Unfinished Legion
A production line creates empty armor bodies whenever conflict occurs nearby. Stopping the line requires cooling the forge, but a haven depends on its heat. Redirecting production can create defenders, workers, or a future occupying force. The first completed armor speaks with the voice of whoever shed the most blood in the chamber.
Chapter 61 — Tier 4 Operations: Isles of Eternal Radiance
Director Purpose
Eternal Radiance replaces darkness with dangerous visibility. Nothing hides easily, reflections become unreliable witnesses, and protection from light is a consumable resource. The tier should challenge assumptions that illumination equals safety or truth.
Core Pressures
Exposure. Radiance depletes shields, reveals marks, and changes creatures or equipment over time.
Reflection. Mirrors, mist seas, crystal surfaces, and doubles complicate identity and line of sight.
Distance. Floating islets and moving bridges make positioning and transport central.
Publicity. Actions are easily witnessed, recorded, refracted, or interpreted by multiple factions.
Radiance as Resource
Light should not be only damage. It charges vials, reveals inscriptions, purifies some contamination, powers constructs, and allows impossible crops. Players choose where to spend shade and where to accept exposure for gain. A Resource team may cultivate Radiant Blooms while Combat protects moving cover and Research aligns a lens.
Encounters
Use mobile platforms, reflected targets, line-of-sight puzzles, and creatures that split or merge according to illumination. Avoid giving every enemy radiant immunity. Let characters who invested in protection feel prepared, then challenge supply and positioning rather than nullifying the investment.
Communities
Tier 4 havens can be sun-forges, bridge-keeper monasteries, floating gardens, or settlements living in the permanent shadow of larger islands. Their laws may regulate mirrors, exposed names, artificial darkness, or ownership of charged light. A criminal market in shade can be as valuable as a market in relics.
Revelation Ceiling
Reflections may show older versions of the tier without current Pact monuments. A mirror can recognize a Bearer or Signatory title while failing to reflect a public deity’s symbol. Present these as contradictions in record and image, not as direct historical narration.
Gate Preparation
Gor Vekk, Architect of Ends, should make the Expedition decide what a perfect conclusion costs. The three sun-shards can represent beginnings, continuations, and endings. How the party charges them should influence whether the Guardian seeks to complete, preserve, or erase the Expedition’s current story.
Sample Floor Seed: The Island That Shows Tomorrow
A small islet reflects the party returning from the tier with one member missing. Each visit changes the identity of the absent figure. The vision can be altered by moving light, but every alteration makes another possible future more likely in the behavior of local constructs.
Chapter 62 — Tier 5 Operations: Sanctum of Echoing Choirs
Director Purpose
The Sanctum is the psychological and logistical wall of the upper descent. It should feel beautiful enough to invite surrender and dangerous enough that silence, music, memory, and composure become tactical concerns. The Bar of Last Refuge is a cultural decision point, not a magical boundary.
Core Pressures
Sound discipline. Volume, pitch, rhythm, interruption, and silence all carry consequences.
Composure. The tier can calm characters into passivity or amplify private thoughts into public sound.
Accumulated wear. By Tier 5, damaged gear, strained alliances, and Opening hours matter as much as new hazards.
Witnessed memory. Echoes preserve conversations, deaths, vows, and performances beyond their original context.
Running Sound Fairly
Describe what characters hear and how the environment responds. Allow experimentation. Players should be able to map acoustic behavior with thrown stones, instruments, cantrips, controlled speech, or silence. Do not require real-world singing talent. Character skill and discovered sequences govern results.
The Bar of Last Refuge
Build the Bar as a place where successful people choose to stop. It needs expensive repair, memorial walls, last-route brokers, retired delvers, and stories of deeper failures. NPCs should treat withdrawal as professionalism, not cowardice. The party’s choice to continue becomes more meaningful when respectable alternatives exist.
Revelation Ceiling
Echoes may preserve titles, conversations, and operational phrases from before accepted history. Some echoes can imply that the First Choir was responding to something below rather than creating the sound itself. The party may begin to suspect that public worship was layered onto an older resonance system.
Gate Preparation
Choralith, Voice Below, should test whether the Expedition can create a shared signal without erasing individual voices. The four-note Canticle sequence should be necessary but insufficient. Each team may need to hold a distinct note, memory, rhythm, or silence while the Guardian attempts to harmonize them into obedience.
Sample Floor Seed: The Mercy Chorus
A chamber sings every reason the Expedition should turn back, using the voices of people who love them. The song is factually accurate but strategically selective. Silencing it destroys valuable preserved memories; answering it strengthens individual resolve but reveals private motives to the whole company.
Chapter 63 — Tier 6 Operations: Crossroads of the Shattered Thread
Director Purpose
Tier 6 is the last region where stable small civilizations remain normal enough to plan around. Its broken Weave-paths make route knowledge political power. Choices create temporary reality, maps require anchors, and different groups may inhabit incompatible versions of the same place.
Core Pressures
Route instability. Paths exist according to timing, witnesses, carried anchors, or previous choices.
Causal disagreement. Two truthful accounts can describe different outcomes because routes diverged.
Political navigation. Civilizations control anchors, crossing rights, and maps that function only under local assumptions.
Separation. Teams may occupy neighboring but non-identical versions of a node.
Civilizations
Build at least two settlements with different methods of stabilizing reality. One may use public records and synchronized bells; another uses living guides who memorize routes; a third may bind crossings through family lineage or legal recognition. None should possess complete truth. Their rivalry should create practical route choices rather than abstract philosophy alone.
Mapping the Shattered Thread
A map needs an anchor statement: who drew it, from which direction, carrying what, under which agreement, and after which event. A copied map can fail because the user does not share those conditions. Reconnaissance and Research can translate conditions rather than merely reproduce lines.
Revelation Ceiling
Tier 6 can establish rung three: the Pact governs and stabilizes mechanisms it did not originate. A civic archive may contain a treaty in which a Pact Signatory assumes control of preexisting crossings. This is strong evidence, but it still does not explain the Silent Ones or the prison’s purpose.
Gate Preparation
Lumen of the Shattered Thread should test whether the Expedition can choose one path without denying the reality of paths it did not take. The Guardian may split the party into outcome-echoes, force sacrifices of possible futures, or require several civilizations to recognize a shared crossing.
Sample Floor Seed: The City of Two Afternoons
Half the city remembers a treaty signed this morning; the other half is still preparing to negotiate it. Both versions occupy the same streets and can affect each other through designated witnesses. The Expedition can profit from the discrepancy, stabilize one history, or construct a costly agreement both versions can inherit.
Chapter 64 — Tier 7 Operations: Pactbound Arena
Director Purpose
Tier 7 turns exact words, public promises, and chosen obligations into terrain. The Arena is not merely a sequence of gladiatorial fights. It is a jurisdiction where conflict becomes contract and spectatorship gives force to claims.
Core Pressures
Oath accumulation. Every promise can become leverage, restriction, protection, or fuel.
Audience. Unseen or remembered witnesses may validate terms and punish private reinterpretation.
Escalating stakes. Victories attract stronger challengers, broader claims, and more valuable offers.
Identity as office. Titles, team roles, reputations, and inherited obligations may matter more than personal names.
Running Bargains
Write important terms down. State what each side believes the wording means and what mechanism or witness enforces it. Allow players to exploit language when they earned the interpretation, but give intelligent NPCs the same opportunity. Do not spring clauses that no character could perceive or understand.
Arena Encounters
Each encounter should contain a contest and a term. The contest may be combat, endurance, testimony, rescue, or public refusal. The term determines what the winner can claim. A party can win the fight and lose the legal result if it ignored the stated objective.
Revelation Ceiling
The Arena can preserve signatures from offices older than the Divine Pact. A contract may show Calan’thir and Asmodeus assuming responsibilities rather than creating the jurisdiction. This can strongly support rung three and prepare the party for the Interstice.
Gate Preparation
Xarog, the Pactbound Furnace, should force the Expedition to decide which promises it is willing to make permanent. The Furnace may burn away false, impossible, or contradictory obligations while empowering those willingly accepted. Passage should leave at least one binding term that matters in Tier 8.
Sample Floor Seed: The Empty Grandstand
An arena has no visible audience, yet applause strengthens any declaration made after a victory. The applause belongs to prior Expeditions whose names were removed from public record. Accepting their acclaim grants power and transfers part of their unfinished claim against the Pact.
Chapter 65 — Operating the Old God Interstice
Director Purpose
The Interstice is the campaign’s break in genre and certainty. Preparation still matters, but ordinary tier assumptions stop being sufficient. The party should recognize patterns from earlier revelations while confronting evidence that the Crucible’s deepest purpose is containment rather than trial alone.
Build Three Layers
1. Perceptible layer. What characters directly experience: impossible space, dream, failing boundary, audience, pursuit, or judgment.
2. Operational layer. What they must do to cross: restore anchors, carry a memory, refuse a bargain, maintain difference, or surrender a relic.
3. Interpretive layer. What the event suggests about the Old God, the Pact, and the underlying prison. Keep at least two plausible readings alive.
The Old God Is Not a Lore Dispenser
Speech may be fragmented, symbolic, translated through the characters, or constrained by containment. Even truthful statements can lack context. Give answers to questions the party earned, but make the being care about its own condition, not about explaining the setting neatly.
Encounter Pressure
Use identity, memory, and route pressure more than raw damage. A character can temporarily forget a class title, exchange memories with another, carry a dead rival’s fear, or become visible to something deeper. Every consequence must remain playable and should have a method of adaptation or recovery.
Rung Four Contact
The Interstice is an appropriate place for the party to experience the operating cycle directly. They may see Gate victories routed as resonance, relics replenished, conflict patterns stabilizing a boundary, or a moment when silence causes containment to weaken. This is contact, not complete understanding.
Leaving the Interstice
Ask each player what certainty their character lost and what difference they refuse to surrender. Record both. Tier 8 should immediately echo those answers through reflections, oaths, or delayed consequences. The crossing creates a new campaign language.
Sample Interstice: The Room With One History
The party enters a chamber where every disagreement, alternate choice, and private memory is gradually rewritten into one shared account. The room becomes safer as differences disappear. To escape, the characters must preserve contradictory truths, willingly carry one another’s memories, and accept that no single account will be complete.
Chapter 66 — Tier 8 Operations: Duskshard Pools
Director Purpose
Duskshard explores delayed identity. Reflections lag, retain discarded possibilities, and sometimes act after the character has moved on. The tier should make players consider what follows them from prior choices without turning every reflection into a hostile clone.
Core Pressures
Delayed consequence. An action may manifest later in a reflection, pool, ally, or route.
Identity drift. Names, oaths, injuries, and memories may appear differently across surfaces.
Cooling light. Radiance becomes residue, metal, prayer, or crystallized dusk rather than direct brilliance.
Uncertain succession. A reflection can claim to be the rightful continuation of a person, object, or Expedition.
Running Reflections
Establish what a surface reflects before breaking the rule. One pool shows intention, another shows the last completed action, another shows the self that made a rejected choice. Players should be able to learn and use these differences. Arbitrary deceptive mirrors quickly become meaningless.
Communities and Remnants
Durable settlements are exceptional. Use oath-protected monasteries, reflection enclaves, survivors who rotate identities, or temporary camps around stable dusk wells. Their continued existence should depend on a bargain or procedure the party may need to understand.
Revelation Ceiling
Tier 8 can show the Heartcell interpreting identity as a pattern rather than a soul-name. Reflections may reveal routing marks, reward echoes, or discarded versions of Gate outcomes. The party can understand that the machine records difference because difference has operational value.
Gate Preparation
Eirfax, the Dusk Crowned, should test which version of the Expedition deserves continuity. The Crown may offer to remove regret, duplicate a successful self, or abandon the burden carried from the Interstice. Passage should require choosing identity without erasing alternatives.
Sample Floor Seed: The Camp That Returned First
The party finds its own camp already established, occupied by exhausted versions of its members who claim to have failed farther below. Their equipment is real, their injuries are consistent, and one carries a relic the party has not yet found. Helping them may create the future they warn against.
Chapter 67 — Tier 9 Operations: Veiled Iron Spiral
Director Purpose
The Spiral compresses distance, memory, and strategic certainty. Progress feels repetitive because the tier hides how much has changed between turns. The challenge is to maintain orientation and purpose when evidence of advancement is deliberately obscured.
Core Pressures
False progress. The party moves without knowing whether it descended, circled, or returned.
Compression. Time, space, supplies, and personal histories may become crowded together.
Concealment. The tier withholds routes, pursuers, exits, and the scale of structures.
Endurance of purpose. The Spiral tests why the Expedition continues when milestones vanish.
Mapping the Spiral
Ordinary distance marks are unreliable. Use changes in material, carried resonance, wounds, oaths, temperature, and external clocks as anchors. A map should record transformations rather than turns. Reconnaissance may prove that two identical corridors occur at different depths because one changes an object or memory differently.
Encounters
Foes should exploit approach and retreat: iron hunters that attack only on repeated routes, compressed swarms that unfold when given space, or enemies encountered at several points in their own timeline. Keep objectives concrete despite surreal presentation: reach an anchor, preserve a record, stop a compression, or rescue a separated team.
Revelation Ceiling
Tier 9 can reveal that the Heartcell measures difference across repeated states and uses history as a stabilizing dimension. The Matrix becomes inferable as a reverse chain: when distinctions collapse, route, time, identity, and will become harder to maintain.
Gate Preparation
Threxus, the Iron Spiral, should test whether the Expedition can define progress without relying on distance. The party may need to demonstrate changes it caused, people it preserved, truths it carried, and choices that cannot be repeated. A purely circular strategy should strengthen the Guardian.
Sample Floor Seed: The Siege on Every Turn
A haven is attacked each time the Spiral completes a coil. The attackers are the same force at different moments of preparation. Defending one turn changes the next, but the haven’s leaders remember only the most recent version. The party must create evidence that survives compression.
Chapter 68 — Tier 10 Operations: Silencebound Void
Director Purpose
Silencebound is not merely quiet. It is the removal of distinctions by which characters know they are present, connected, and capable of choosing. The final tier should pressure communication and identity while preserving enough structure that decisions remain meaningful.
Core Pressures
Signal loss. Speech, gesture, magic, tracks, and memory cease to reach others reliably.
Unspoken intent. The tier reacts to choices before they become action or language.
Erasure. Names, histories, differences, and relationships may become easier to surrender than to maintain.
Final temptation. Stillness offers relief from conflict, grief, uncertainty, and responsibility.
Running Silence
Do not forbid players from speaking to one another out of character for long periods. Translate the fiction into constraints: written plans may arrive incomplete, only one word crosses a boundary, a character can communicate emotion but not detail, or teams must choose signals before separation. The table should solve communication problems, not sit in actual silence until bored.
No Ordinary Settlements
Any population here is a final remnant, protected enclave, transformed chorus, trapped office, or deliberate exception. Its existence must answer what it surrendered to remain. Comfort should feel precious and conditional, not like another roadside inn.
Revelation Ceiling
Tier 10 may fully expose the Heartcell, the Ul’nak, and the Matrix if the campaign earned it. Revelation should occur through operation and consequence: the party sees what conflict preserves, what the prison contains, and what perfect stillness would erase. Avoid reducing the truth to a single villain’s monologue.
The Marquis of Silence
The Marquis should understand the characters’ exhaustion and offer an end that is genuinely attractive: no more loss, no more contradiction, no more contested history. The confrontation tests whether the party can preserve difference without romanticizing endless suffering. Victory may involve defeating, replacing, persuading, binding, or transforming the Marquis and the role it occupies.
Sample Floor Seed: The Last Conversation
Each character may speak only one final sentence that another person will remember. Everything else said in the chamber is erased from history. The route opens when the Expedition proves that identity can survive incomplete communication. The sentences become part of the final confrontation and the next Cycle’s mythology.

Using the Gallery

Building a Site in Five Lines
1. Function. What Expeditions can reliably obtain or accomplish there.
2. Dependency. What the site needs from the dungeon, surface, or another faction.
3. Law. The rule outsiders are most likely to violate.
4. Face. The person who interprets the site to the party.
5. Change. What can improve, corrupt, destroy, or transform the site through play.
Site Improvement
Allow Expeditions to invest money, salvage, labor, reputation, maps, and specialists in sites. Improvements should reduce future costs or unlock procedures: faster travel, safer rest, better treatment, research, storage, or legal recognition. Every improvement also increases what can be lost or contested.
Chapter 73 — Endgame Frameworks
A Decennium Descent campaign can end before Floor 100 without failure. The endgame begins when the Expedition can make a choice that changes the relationship between Aethel, the Opening, and the Heartcell. Choose an endgame framework that follows the campaign’s discoveries and values.
Preserve the Pact
The party concludes that controlled dissonance remains necessary and works to repair, reform, or inherit the existing system. The conflict becomes who controls it, what suffering is acceptable, and whether the truth can remain hidden. This ending preserves the world but may perpetuate exploitation.
Transform the Engine
The Expedition seeks another source of sustaining difference: voluntary creation, plural memory, ritualized competition, art, dreams, or distributed resonance that reduces the need for war and predation. The transformation should require proof, sacrifice, and a new institution capable of surviving future Cycles.
Break the Prison
The party frees or destroys what the Heartcell contains. This is not automatically liberation or doom. Decide what the Old Ones are, what the Pact concealed correctly, and what consequences follow. The campaign becomes about surviving a new cosmology rather than simply winning a boss fight.
Seal the Truth
The characters may learn enough to act and choose to bury the evidence. This can be compassionate, cowardly, strategic, or corrupt depending on what they protect. The endgame resolves who inherits the secret and what safeguards prevent the same knowledge from becoming merely another faction weapon.
Replace the Marquis
The final role may require a bearer. A character, rival, deity, Old God fragment, or collective institution can become the new boundary against stillness. Replacement should change the bearer profoundly and create a legacy for the next Cycle.
Refuse the Binary
The party may reject both perfect stillness and perpetual engineered conflict. This is the most demanding ending because it requires the characters to preserve difference without making suffering sacred. Use every relationship, haven, rival, relic, and promise accumulated during the campaign as evidence that difference can be maintained through more than war.
Final Questions for the Marquis
Which difference would each character preserve even if it causes pain?
Which conflict has become unnecessary, and who profits from keeping it?
What truth can the world survive knowing now?
Who has the authority to choose for future Cycles?
What part of the existing system must remain until a replacement actually works?
What will the characters sacrifice that is theirs to give, rather than someone else’s?
Endgame Standard
The final choice should be understandable from evidence accumulated during play, should permit more than one defensible answer, and should visibly involve the people and institutions the Expedition affected. A cosmic ending that ignores the campaign’s human consequences is incomplete.

Adjudication Extras
Chases: pick a clock (3–5 ticks). Each fail advances environmental doom (collapse, spore, heat). Success spends a period or a resource.

Session Opening Script
State the current hour, floor, active objective, known extraction route, highest pressure, and one visible change in the world. Ask each team what it is doing during the opening period. Then begin with a decision, not a recap lecture.

Canon Guardrails
Publicly, Calan’thir and Asmodeus built the Crucible under the Divine Pact.
Director-secret: the Silent Ones, later called the Ul’nak, built the underlying Heartcell.
Treasure, conflict, death, and Gate victories feed the Heartcell’s operation.
The Matrix of Oblivion is the reverse collapse from difference and life toward stillness.
The Opening lasts 2400 hours; the Horn sounds at hour 1200.
The Decennium has 100 floors, ten tiers, and ten canonical Gate Guardians.
The Old God Interstice lies between Tiers 7 and 8 and is not another tier.
Tier 6 is the last normal depth for stable small civilizations.
The Fifteenfold Pantheon and Great Wheel are mortal religious frameworks, not objective Director cosmology.
Player-facing rules in the Player Rulebook take precedence over summaries in this manual.
